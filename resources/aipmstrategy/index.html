The strategy question isn't "can we build it?" — it's "will it still matter once the platform ships it?"

The premise

Strategy is now mostly a question about what won't be absorbed

Every other decision on this page is downstream of that one.

For twenty years, product strategy asked whether you could build something and whether anyone wanted it. AI adds a third question that dominates both: will this still be yours in eighteen months, or will the platform ship it as a feature?

The pattern is not new. Relational databases were a moat and became a commodity. Cloud infrastructure was a moat and became a utility. Each time, the layer that felt central became substrate, and durable advantage moved to whatever sat on top and grew more useful with use. Foundation models are the next layer doing this — and doing it faster, because inference prices have fallen by orders of magnitude and open-weight models now trail the closed frontier by months rather than years.

So the discipline is subtraction before addition. Before you argue about what to build, work out which parts of your intended surface area have a half-life measured in release cycles. Then spend your scarce engineering time on the rest.

The rule of thumb that survives contact with a steering committee: if a competent competitor could replicate it in a weekend with the same API, it isn't strategy — it's a feature you happen to have first.

Three questions, in order

The sequence that saves money

  1. Is the capability reliable enough today? Not in principle — on your actual task, probed with real examples. Capability is discovered, not specified.
  2. Does owning it differentiate us? Would a customer pay a premium specifically because we built it rather than bought it? If not, it's operational hygiene.
  3. Does it survive the next model generation? If the answer is "our advantage is that we use a good model", you have no answer.

Most capabilities resolve on question one or two. Run full cost modelling only on the two or three that carry real strategic weight.

The build–buy line

It isn't one decision, it's six

The single most common strategy error is applying one doctrine across the whole stack. "We build our AI" and "we buy our AI" are both wrong answers, because the right call at the model layer is almost never the right call at the evaluation layer. Decide per layer, deliberately, and expect a hybrid.

The shape most enterprises land on

Buy the foundation — models, infrastructure, observability, generic connectors. Build the intelligence layer — domain logic, proprietary context, decision rules, evaluation. Partner for specialised execution where deep expertise matters but doesn't need to live permanently on your payroll. Commodity gets rented, differentiation gets owned. The useful discipline is being able to say which is which, per layer, with a reason.

What the number can't tell you

Cost decides ties, not strategy. If the capability is genuinely differentiating, a more expensive build can still be the right call — and if it isn't, being cheaper to build is not a reason to build it. Run this model on the two or three capabilities that carry strategic weight and buy the rest without a spreadsheet.

The speed argument is real

If you need to be live in six weeks and a vendor can do it, an eight-month build is a strategic liability regardless of the three-year total. Buying first to validate, then building the layer that proved differentiating, is a legitimate sequence — and usually a cheaper way to learn than a fourteen-month custom build that never validated demand.

Sequencing

A roadmap of gates, not dates

Feature-with-a-date rows break for AI work, for three structural reasons: capability is discovered rather than specified, progress isn't linear — getting from roughly 70% to 90% task success routinely takes longer than getting from zero to 70% — and done isn't a state but a distribution that keeps moving after launch. The reframe worth stealing: an AI roadmap commits to learning something by a date, not to something working by a date. Put hard dates only on process steps you control.

The rule for the Later column

Anything sitting in Later purely because the model isn't good enough yet gets a revisit-at-next-generation tag, an owner, and a standing calendar entry. Half the value of an AI roadmap is knowing which parked items to un-park the week a new model lands — and being the team that checks within days rather than months.

The rule for the Now column

Every item carries a quality bar and a current eval score, and an explicit kill option. If an item can't state what number would make you stop, it isn't a gate — it's a wish. This is also the honest answer to "when will it be ready": a range tied to eval evidence, forecast from measured trajectory the way you'd forecast a conversion curve.

The event you don't schedule

A new model lands. Now what?

Two or three times a year a release changes your feasibility frontier, your cost structure, or both. Teams that handle this well have a rehearsed drill. Teams that don't spend a fortnight arguing in a Slack thread and then migrate under pressure when the old version sunsets.

Last thing

Assume the platform catches up

The uncomfortable version of everything above is that a large share of what feels like real product work today — orchestration glue, tool connectors, retrieval scaffolding, generic guardrails — is being steadily absorbed into platform primitives. Teams spend months building it and then watch it collapse into table stakes.

That's not an argument against building. It's an argument for being precise about which months you spend. The teams that hold up over multiple model generations tend to have invested in the same unglamorous places: their own evaluation pipelines, their proprietary data and the feedback loop around it, and the workflows they've become the system of record for. None of those get shipped away in a release note.

A useful closing question for any roadmap review: if the model layer became free tomorrow, what would still be worth something? Fund that.
