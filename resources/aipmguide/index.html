The premise

The job changed when the output stopped being the same twice

Everything else follows from this one property.

Classical product management rests on a quiet assumption: given the same input, the system does the same thing. That assumption is load-bearing. It's why you can write an acceptance criterion, why QA can sign off, why a bug is reproducible, why "it works" is a binary, and why a demo is evidence.

An AI feature breaks all of it at once. The same input can produce a different output on consecutive runs. Correct is a spectrum, not a bit. A feature that is excellent for 94% of queries can destroy trust because the 6% clusters on your highest-value use case. Quality degrades silently when the model version changes, when the retrieval index goes stale, or when real users drift away from the traffic you tested on. And every single call costs money.

So the AI PM's job is not "PM, but the feature has AI in it." It is a different operating posture: you are making confident product calls on top of a system whose behaviour you can only characterise statistically.

What actually moves

Five load-bearing shifts

  1. Spec → behaviour contract. You stop writing "returns the right answer" and start writing thresholds, confidence bands, and named fallbacks.
  2. QA → evals. Test suites become graded datasets. You own the definition of good.
  3. Fixed cost → marginal cost. For the first time since 2005, serving one more user has a real price attached.
  4. Ship → keep shipping. Launch is where measurement starts, not where it ends. Nothing you shipped stays shipped.
  5. Usability → trust. The bottleneck on adoption is rarely whether people can use it. It's whether they'll rely on it.
Taxonomy

Four roles wearing the same title

"AI PM" on a job description can mean four materially different jobs, with different skills, different stakeholders, and different pay bands. Before you take the role — or write the req — settle which one it is. Mis-labelling the archetype is the single most common reason an AI PM hire goes badly.

Day to day

Where the week actually goes

The honest version. Same nominal job, same 40 hours — but the allocation moves sharply. Toggle between them and watch which bars grow.

The new standing meeting

Weekly error review: the team sits down with 20–40 real traces where the system did something wrong, reads them line by line, and names the failure modes. This is the highest-leverage hour in an AI PM's week and the one most often skipped.

The new artefact

The golden set — a hand-curated file of representative inputs with agreed-good outputs. It is a product document, not a test fixture, and the PM is usually the right owner because it encodes what "good" means to the customer.

The new escalation

"The model provider deprecated it." Your dependency has a roadmap you don't control and a sunset date you didn't pick. Version pinning, migration evals, and a fallback model are now standing items, not incidents.

The core loop

Eval-driven development

If you learn one thing from this page, learn this loop. It is the scientific method wearing a product hat, and it is the clearest single dividing line between AI PMs who ship reliably and ones who ship demos. Evals are written before the system can pass them — the same instinct as test-driven development, applied to a system that can't be unit-tested.

Sizing it

Start at roughly 100 high-quality goldens and cap around 500. Unlike unit tests, evals cost money and time to run, so you keep the set small and sharp rather than exhaustive. Quality beats volume — a few hundred well-labelled examples is enough to build an effective eval.

The discipline

Every manual review is a missing eval. When a human judges an output, extract that judgement into a rubric, automate the rubric, then evaluate the evaluator. Otherwise the knowledge stays in one person's head and the system regresses the moment they're on leave.

The artefact

What an AI PRD adds

You don't throw out your PRD template. You bolt on the sections that a probabilistic system needs and that a deterministic one never did. Each of these below is a section engineering will otherwise have to invent for themselves — usually badly, usually in a Slack thread, usually after launch.

Two failure modes to avoid: over-specifying model behaviour by trying to script every possible output (you can't — that's the point), and under-specifying failure by writing only the happy path. The useful middle is a behaviour contract: given failure type X, the system does Y, and the user sees Z.

The tradeoff nobody warned you about

Marginal cost came back

SaaS taught a generation of PMs that serving one more user is free. Inference is a real variable cost on every single call, and it does not shrink with scale. Gross margins on AI products commonly land in the 60–70% range rather than the 80–90% SaaS norm. Model this before launch, not after the margin crisis.

Where the money goes
Lever 1 — routing

Within one vendor the spread between the cheap tier and the frontier tier runs 5× or more. Most surfaces don't need frontier intelligence, they need consistency. Send the hard 10–20% to the expensive model and the rest to a small one, and validate the split with evals rather than vibes.

Lever 2 — token discipline

A bloated system prompt is re-sent on every single call. Trimming 4,000 tokens down to 800 cuts input cost by 80% forever. Teams that instrument tokens from launch and audit prompts quarterly typically find 40–60% savings in the first six months with no quality loss — because they're removing accretion, not optimising something already tight.

Measurement

Four layers of metric, and the trap between them

The most common analytical failure in AI product work is reporting a model metric to an executive as though it were a business metric. They are four different altitudes and they move independently — accuracy can rise while retention falls.

The trap

Layers 1 and 2 are the ones you can move in a sprint, so they're the ones teams optimise. Layers 3 and 4 are the ones the business is paying for. If you can't draw a causal line from "F1 improved 4 points" to "advisors accepted 11% more recommendations", you have not yet made a product argument — you've made an engineering observation. Write the line down in the PRD before you build.

Interaction design

Designing for a system that's sometimes wrong

AI UX is the design discipline classical UX never had to address: what does the interface show when the model is uncertain? Users don't expect AI to be perfect — they expect to know when they can rely on it. Confident wrong answers destroy trust; visible uncertainty preserves it.

The autonomy ladder

Autonomy is not a switch, it's a rung you earn. Most teams should target level 2 for v1. Pick a rung deliberately per action, not per product — reading an order status can be autonomous because it's read-only and reversible; issuing a refund is not.

Patterns worth stealing

Field identification

The failure catalogue

AI features fail differently from software features. They rarely throw an exception — they return something plausible and wrong. Learning to name failure modes precisely is most of the skill, because a named failure mode becomes an eval, and an eval becomes a regression gate.

Constraints

Shipping inside a regulated perimeter

In most industries governance is a review gate. In financial services it's a design input — a bank deploying a model in 2026 sits inside model risk management guidance, fair-lending law, prudential supervision, data protection rules, and, for EU exposure, a horizontal AI law with its own penalty regime. No other sector carries that many overlapping regimes at once. The AI PM who treats compliance as a late-stage checkpoint ships six months late.

What moved recently

What it means at your desk

The 2026 shift worth internalising

The principles didn't move; the implementation surface did. With single-shot scoring models, governance asked: was it fair, was it explainable, who was accountable for this decision? With agentic systems that plan, call tools, and act over many steps, the risk surface is no longer the model in isolation — it's the operating environment the model runs inside: what it's allowed to touch, what it logs, where a human can intervene, and how intent is recorded. Recent guidance reads like an operations manual rather than an ethics statement. Your permission surface is your primary safety control.

The bar

What the interview actually tests

The prep that worked in 2023 gets rejected now. Loops have shifted from "explain what a transformer is" to "make a call under uncertainty and show your arithmetic." Behavioural questions got AI-specific: not "tell me about a trade-off" but "describe a time you chose between accuracy and serving latency, and explain the reasoning." Open each one for what a strong answer sounds like.

Getting there

A 90-day ramp that actually works

Whether you're moving into an AI PM seat or trying to level up in one. The bar rose sharply through 2025–26: hiring managers now want shipped artefacts, eval fluency, and a point of view. Certificates move the needle least; a golden set you built yourself moves it most.

The arbitrage

Eval work is unglamorous, so most PMs underinvest in it — which is exactly why it's the differentiator. If you can walk through golden-set design, LLM-as-judge tradeoffs, and regression-suite maintenance in a panel without being coached, you're in the top quartile of candidates. Everything else on your CV is table stakes.

One more thing

The title probably dissolves. The skills don't.

We've been through this title cycle before — Mobile PM, Web3 PM, Growth PM, Data PM. Each had its moment as a separate specialty and each quietly merged back into "just product management" once the tooling became baseline. There's a reasonable argument that the AI PM title goes the same way inside two years.

That's not a reason to skip any of this. It's the opposite. When the title dissolves, what's left is the residue: the ability to specify behaviour you can't fully control, to define good precisely enough to automate the judgement, to hold a cost-quality-latency tradeoff in your head while a stakeholder asks for all three, and to say "we don't ship this yet" with evidence rather than instinct.

Those become table stakes for every PM. The people who built them early just get there first.
