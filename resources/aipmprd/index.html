The premise

Five assumptions your PRD template is quietly making

A classical PRD is a good document resting on five load-bearing beliefs. An AI feature removes all five at once, which is why the template doesn't feel wrong so much as insufficient — engineering reads it, nods, and then makes a dozen consequential decisions you never wrote down.

The reframe

A classical requirement describes an output. An AI requirement describes a behaviour, a way of measuring it, a bar it must clear, and what happens when it doesn't. Same intent, four times the surface — because the three extra parts are the decisions someone has to make regardless. The only question is whether you make them or they get made for you at 4pm on release day.

Acceptance criteria

Three kinds of check, and when each is honest

Not everything needs a judge. Reach for the cheapest evaluator that is trustworthy for the criterion, because cheap checks run on every commit and expensive ones don't. Most good AI PRDs use all three, explicitly labelled.

The rule that saves arguments

Anything objective gets a deterministic check, always. Was the date parsed correctly, did the JSON validate, does every figure appear in the retrieved source, is the client ID one this adviser is entitled to see. These are free, instant, never drift, and they catch the failures that actually get you in trouble.

The rule that saves credibility

A judged criterion without a calibration number is decoration. State the agreement between your automated judge and human labels in the PRD itself. If you can't, the honest clause is "human-rated on a sample of N per release" — slower, but true.

The section everyone skips

Failure modes, in three columns

For every way the feature can fail: how the system detects it, what the user sees, how it recovers. Three things, every time, no exceptions. This is the section most PRDs omit and most post-mortems demand — and it's also the cheapest to write, because your team already knows the answers, they've just never been asked to write them down.

FailureDetectUser seesRecover

Copy this table structure directly into your PRD and fill a row per failure mode you've actually observed in traces. Rows invented at a desk are worth about a third of rows harvested from production — start with the four or five you've seen, and add one every time error review turns up something new.

For anything agentic

Permissions belong in the spec, per action

The permission surface is the primary safety control, which makes it a product decision rather than an implementation detail. Assign autonomy per action and tie it to reversibility, never per product. Reading an order status can be autonomous because it's read-only; issuing a refund cannot.

Write the escalation rule too

Separately from per-action autonomy, state when the agent should stop and ask regardless: low confidence, an anomaly, a value threshold, an irreversibility flag, an approaching SLA. A useful target is escalation on roughly 5–15% of tasks. Too frequent and users lose faith in the thing; too rare and it will eventually make a very confident, very wrong decision unsupervised.

After you write it

    
It's a living document, on three clocks

    
An AI PRD that isn't revised is out of date within a quarter — a model version bump, a new failure mode from error review, a routing change. But not all of it moves at the same speed. Split it by cadence so the review is fifteen minutes rather than a rewrite.

  

  

  

    Keep it lean
    
Three audiences read this document and none of them read all of it. Leadership needs the strategic tier; cross-functional teams need the behaviour and quality tiers; engineering and operations need the operational detail. Put the core in the main document and push eval samples, conversation examples, competitor capability matrices and the AI-literacy primer into appendices. A spec's job is to align humans — length is not evidence of thought, and post-assistant it's often evidence of the opposite.

Last thing

    
The spec is the part that survives

  

  

    
Prompts get rewritten. Models get deprecated. The orchestration layer you built gets absorbed into a platform primitive. What persists across all of it is the artefact that says what good means here, how you'd know, and what the system does when it falls short.

    
That's a strange thing to realise about a document that most teams treat as overhead. But the behaviour contract, the failure table, the permission surface and the eval spec are the only parts of an AI feature that a model upgrade can't invalidate — they're the definition the new model gets measured against. Write them once, properly, and every future model decision gets cheaper.

    
Which is the honest reason to spend an afternoon on this rather than a paragraph: not process, leverage.
