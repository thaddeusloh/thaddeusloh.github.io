A reading of an ethnographic classic

A community that lost the language and kept the ancestors

Tan Chee Beng spent the late 1970s among the Baba of Melaka asking a question that sounds simple and isn't: if you no longer speak Chinese, dress Malay, eat something in between, and everyone around you says you've lost the plot — in what sense are you still Chinese? His answer reshaped how Southeast Asian hybridity gets talked about.

Before you open it

This is a doctoral thesis that grew up into the standard reference. It is careful, granular, and occasionally dry — Tan counts households, tabulates loanwords, and reconstructs funeral sequences step by step. What redeems the density is that every descriptive chapter is quietly doing argumentative work, and the argument is genuinely good.

Two things to hold in mind. First, Tan is an insider-ish outsider: a Chinese Malaysian anthropologist who speaks Baba Malay, working on a community that other Chinese Malaysians tended to treat as a curiosity or a joke. That position gives the book unusual access and an unmistakable protectiveness. Second, the fieldwork is from roughly 1977 to the early 1980s. That predates the Melaka UNESCO listing, the Peranakan museum boom, the television dramas, and the whole heritage economy that now shapes what "Peranakan" means. The book is not a description of the Peranakan world you can visit today. It is the baseline you measure that world against, which is arguably more useful.

One structural note worth knowing before you start: chapters 1 and 2 set up terms, history and demography. Chapters 3 through 8 are the actual argument, and they build. Chapter 3 states the thesis; 4 through 7 test it across four domains; 8 asks what happens next. If you read the introduction and then jump to chapter 3, you lose very little.

Going through it chapter by chapter

What each part is doing, and the thing in it most likely to stay with you.

—INTRO

Ethnicity, acculturation, and why they aren't the same thing

The theoretical setup, and the hinge the whole book swings on: Tan separates acculturation (adopting another group's cultural traits) from assimilation (ceasing to be a distinct group). Everyone in 1979 assumed the first led inevitably to the second. Tan says no, and spends 250 pages proving it with funerals and kinship charts.

Worth knowing. He is working in the tradition of Fredrik Barth — ethnic groups are defined by the boundaries they maintain, not by the cultural contents inside them. Once you see this frame, the whole book unlocks, and so do a lot of arguments you've probably had about what makes someone "properly" Chinese.

1CHAPTER

What the word "Baba" actually means

A whole chapter on a label, which sounds pedantic until you realise how much confusion it clears. Baba, Peranakan, Straits Chinese and Nyonya are not synonyms and never were. Peranakan is the broad term for locally-born descendants of immigrants — there are Chitty Indian and Jawi Peranakan communities too. Straits Chinese was largely a colonial-legal status: Chinese born as British subjects in the Straits Settlements. Baba is the cultural category: Chinese descendants who stopped using Chinese languages and speak Baba Malay.

Worth knowing. Because the category is cultural rather than ancestral, it has a leaky edge Tan is honest about: descendants of much later immigrants who lost Chinese by the third generation also get called Baba. The community is not a sealed bloodline, which undercuts most of the romantic origin stories told about it.

Also note the word's double life — respectful address in one mouth, a mild sneer in another. That ambivalence is data, not noise.

2CHAPTER

How the community formed, and where it lives

History and demography: settlement patterns in Melaka, household counts, distribution across town and kampung. Tan is deflationary about origins. The community's own favourite story reaches back to a fifteenth-century Ming princess and her retinue arriving at the Malacca Sultanate; the documentary evidence points instead to a long, unglamorous process of Chinese men marrying local women and raising locally-rooted families, mostly under Dutch and then British Melaka.

Worth knowing. The status reversal is the striking part. In the colonial period the Baba were among the most prosperous and politically connected Chinese in Malaya — English-educated, brokered between colonisers and locals. After independence the exact traits that made them useful became liabilities: too Malay for the Chinese, not Malay enough to count as Bumiputera. Very few communities get to be an elite and a punchline within one lifetime.

3CHAPTER

Ethnic identity — the central claim

The load-bearing chapter. Tan argues the Baba hold a double identity: they are, in his formulation, both "Baba" and "Chinese" — the second is not diluted by the first. He works through the overt markers people use to recognise each other and be recognised: sarong kebaya, the food, dondang sayang, the speech, and the dense web of who knows whose family.

He borrows the idea of situational ethnicity to explain what looks like inconsistency. Which identity a person foregrounds depends on who is in the room — Baba among Chinese, Chinese among Malays, Malaysian abroad. This isn't fence-sitting; it's how multi-layered identity normally operates, and most people reading this will recognise the manoeuvre from their own lives.

Worth knowing. There's real pain documented here. The Baba were mocked by immigrant Chinese for exactly what had once made them powerful. Tan records the sting without turning the community into victims, which is a hard tonal line to walk.

4CHAPTER

Baba Malay

The linguistic chapter, and the most quoted one outside anthropology. Baba Malay has a Malay-based vocabulary sitting on a Hokkien substrate, with the borrowing heavily concentrated in specific domains. Tan documents which ones, and the pattern is the argument.

Worth knowing. The Hokkien survives precisely where Chineseness is being transmitted — kinship terms, ritual, festivals, household and food vocabulary. A community can switch language wholesale and still keep Chinese words for who your father's elder brother is and what you burn for him. That single distribution fact does more argumentative work than any amount of assertion about identity.

Note that Tan treats Baba Malay as a Malay dialect; later linguists have argued forcefully for creole. If you care about that debate, read him for the data and go elsewhere for the classification.

5CHAPTER

Customs and religion

Ancestor worship, deities, temples, the festival calendar. The expectation going in is that this is where you'd find the most hybridity. The finding is the opposite: in religion the Baba are substantially continuous with other Chinese in Malaysia, sharing the same folk religious world.

Worth knowing. Tan's reading is that ritual absorbs the load that language used to carry. Chinese religious practice becomes the working substitute for Chinese speech as the guarantor of Chinese identity — which is why it gets maintained with such care rather than drifting. If you only read two chapters of this book, make it this one and the next.

6CHAPTER

Death rituals

Funerary practice in close detail — the most conservative domain in the whole culture, organised around filial piety. Tan finds the Baba are often more punctilious about traditional rites than immigrant Chinese families, not less. Malay influence has touched the surface of some practices; the underlying cosmology and ethos are largely intact.

Worth knowing. This is where the ethnic boundary becomes visible and hard. Islam and Chinese religion function as the mutually exclusive markers — conversion effectively means leaving the community, and Tan shows how families understand that. Language was negotiable. Dress was negotiable. What you do at a graveside was not. That asymmetry explains a great deal about ethnic politics in the region.

7CHAPTER

Kinship, marriage, and social organisation

Households, descent, matchmaking, associations, and the famous old Baba wedding with its long sequence of rites — the hairdo ritual, the ritual specialist who guides the couple, the days of ceremony. Tan also traces intermarriage patterns and how they shifted across generations.

Worth knowing. The wedding is the one place where the hybridity is unmistakable and celebrated rather than defensive — Chinese ritual structure carried out in Malay-influenced dress, language and idiom. It's also the practice that has been most thoroughly turned into heritage spectacle since Tan wrote, so his account is a useful record of it as lived family obligation rather than performance.

Watch the position of nyonyas in this chapter. Tan gives more material on women's roles than his contemporaries did, though a reader today will want more than he provides.

8CHAPTER

Ethnic change, and what comes next

The closing chapter looks forward from around 1980: education in English, Malay and increasingly Mandarin; younger Baba re-acquiring Chinese their grandparents lost; the pull of a broader Chinese Malaysian identity on one side and a national Malaysian one on the other; the community shrinking as a distinct group even as interest in it grows.

Worth knowing. There's a political charge under the surface here that reviewers picked up at the time. By showing a community that genuinely fused Malay and Chinese culture and still ended up disadvantaged by a racialised policy framework, Tan makes an implicit case against the logic of the Bumiputera system without ever writing a polemic. Reading it in 2026, the chapter's real interest is how much it got right and where it under-predicted — he could not have foreseen the heritage revival that made Peranakan identity commercially valuable and therefore, complicatedly, viable again.

The one idea to take away

Cultural change and identity loss are separate processes. A group can shed language, dress and cuisine and still hold a boundary — as long as something keeps doing the work the lost markers used to do. For the Baba, that something was ritual.

That's portable well beyond Melaka. It applies to diaspora communities everywhere, to religious minorities, and — if you're inclined to stretch it — to any institution that changes its practices and worries it's losing itself. The question is never "how much has changed?" It's "what is carrying the boundary now?"

Where it shows its age

Being fair to a forty-year-old monograph means naming the gaps. The demographic tables are historical documents at this point, not current data. The prose still carries its dissertation origins — signposting, literature-positioning, and a good deal of detail that serves the thesis committee more than the reader. Class and economics get thinner treatment than kinship and ritual, which is odd for a community defined partly by its commercial position. And while Tan is better on women than most of his contemporaries, a nyonya-centred account of this culture still had to wait for other writers.

The most consequential absence is simply chronological. Everything that has shaped Peranakan identity since — heritage listing, tourism, museums, popular drama, the Singapore and Malaysian revivals running on different tracks — happens after the last page. Read it as the control group.

So should you read it

Yes, if

  • You want the primary scholarly source rather than another heritage-brochure summary
  • You're interested in how identity survives cultural change, in any context
  • You're working on Southeast Asian Chinese communities and need the baseline everyone cites
  • You like ethnography where the detail is the argument

Probably not, if

  • You want a narrative history or a story-driven read — this is a monograph, and it reads like one
  • You're after current Peranakan life, food culture or the heritage scene
  • You want the Singapore Peranakan trajectory specifically; that diverged under different state policy

What to read alongside it

John Clammer, Straits Chinese Society
The other foundational sociological treatment from roughly the same era. Useful for triangulating Tan's Melaka picture.
Jürgen Rudolph, Reconstructing Identities
A social history of the Babas in Singapore. The obvious comparison case if you want to see how the same community diverged across a border.
Felix Chia, The Babas
Written from inside the community rather than from the discipline. Warmer, looser, and a good counterweight to Tan's analytical distance.
Nala H. Lee's work on Baba Malay grammar
Where to go if chapter 4 leaves you wanting the proper linguistic treatment, including the creole classification Tan doesn't accept.
