The review

A picture book that happens to be written by the people holding the red pen on the global tree assessment.

Most books about trees are written from affection. This one is written from an inventory. Sara Oldfield chairs the IUCN Species Survival Commission’s Global Tree Specialist Group and previously ran Botanic Gardens Conservation International; Malin Rivers heads conservation prioritisation at BGCI and manages the programme that has been working through the world’s tree species one by one, deciding how close each is to disappearing. The scale of the answer is the quiet premise underneath every page.

That provenance shows in the way the species entries are built. Each gets a sidebar carrying its Red List category, native range, habitat and threats, then a longer piece of prose about what the tree is, what people have done with it, and what is now being done to keep it. It is the structure of a Red List account rewritten so a non-botanist can read it in three minutes, laid over photography good enough to justify the format. There is no glossary of jargon to wade through and no assumed Latin.

The choice that gives the book its shape is the decision to organise the same 60 species three different ways. The first chapter arranges them by the ecosystem they hold up. The second arranges them by what humans extract from them. The third arranges them taxonomically, by family and genus. Read straight through, the effect is triangulation: you meet a tree as a piece of forest architecture, then as a commodity, then as a branch on a family tree that is losing several of its other branches at once. Very few conservation books hold those three framings in one volume without letting one swallow the others.

The weakness is the flip side of the same choice. This is an anthology, not an argument. It accumulates cases rather than building toward a thesis about why the losses are happening or what would actually reverse them, and the closing gestures toward what a reader can do are thinner than the diagnosis that precedes them. Individual accounts are short enough that a species you become invested in ends before you are satisfied. And because the organising principle changes between chapters, the same tree can plausibly belong in two places at once, which occasionally makes the book feel like a well-curated card index rather than a narrative.

None of that undercuts what it does well. It is the rare conservation title that is honest about numbers while still being a genuinely pleasurable object to sit with, and it is far better as a reference than its proportions imply. If you want one book that tells you which trees are in trouble, why, and who is trying to stop it, this is a defensible single choice.

Verdict

In one line
An IUCN Red List with a photographer attached — authoritative, beautiful, and slightly reluctant to argue.
Strongest
Species accounts, threat descriptions, and the three-way structure that keeps recontextualising the same trees.
Weakest
Short entries, little synthesis, and an ending that diagnoses more convincingly than it prescribes.
Read it
By dipping. It rewards a species at a time better than a cover-to-cover run.

What’s in it, chapter by chapter

An introduction, then three main chapters, each subdivided into sections. Below: what each section covers and the point in it most worth carrying away.

Chapter one

Trees and their ecosystems

Trees organised by the habitat they build. Each section describes where the ecosystem occurs, what depends on it, how it is being dismantled, and which species carry the structure.

Section 1.1

Tropical rainforests

The highest-diversity forests and the ones with the most familiar threat profile: selective logging first, then conversion to plantation and agriculture. This is where the book introduces the idea that a rainforest is not a generic green mass but a structure held up by a small number of canopy-dominant species.

Worth carrying away

Bornean ironwood is dense enough to sink in water, which made it the standard timber for houses, bridges and boat keels across Borneo. That density comes from growing extremely slowly — which is exactly why a felled stand cannot be replaced on any commercially interesting timescale.

Section 1.2

Tropical cloud forests

Forests that live inside a narrow altitudinal band of persistent cloud, drawing much of their water directly out of the air. Extremely high endemism: a single mountain range can hold species found on one slope and nowhere else on the planet.

The uncomfortable bit

A warming climate raises the altitude at which cloud forms. The habitat therefore migrates uphill and species have to migrate with it — until they reach the summit and run out of mountain. No protected-area boundary fixes this, which makes it the clearest case in the book of a tree endangered by something nobody is doing on the site itself.

Section 1.3

Tropical dry forests

Seasonally dry woodland and savannah — less photogenic than rainforest, far more converted, and much less protected. Covers the trees that survive months without rain, and pressure from grazing, charcoal production and clearance for cropland.

Worth carrying away

Dry forest is proportionally among the most transformed forest types on earth, precisely because it is easier to clear and farm than wet forest. Attention and protection have followed rainforest imagery rather than the actual conversion figures.

Section 1.4

Temperate and boreal forests

The forests of the northern latitudes and the southern temperate zone, written partly to puncture the assumption that trees in wealthy, well-governed countries are therefore safe.

Worth carrying away

The Bikin River valley in the Russian Far East — part of the Central Sikhote-Alin World Heritage site and Amur tiger habitat — holds Manchurian ash and Mongolian oak that have been hit hard by illegal logging. World Heritage designation and a charismatic predator on the same ground did not stop the trees being taken.

Section 1.5

Islands

The chapter’s emotional peak and its statistical extreme. Islands generate endemics through isolation and then lose them fast: small ranges, tiny populations, introduced goats and rats, and no upslope refuge.

The uncomfortable bit

Island species routinely reach the point where the entire remaining wild population could be counted by one person in an afternoon. Socotra’s dragon tree is the emblem: a population dominated by old individuals, with grazing removing seedlings faster than they establish, so the forest is ageing without replacing itself. A stand can look healthy and be functionally finished.

Chapter two

Trees and their uses

The same subject re-sorted by human demand. This is the chapter that explains most of the threat categories in chapter one: for a large share of these species, the reason they are rare is that they are useful.

Section 2.1

Medicines, poisons and perfumes

Trees harvested for what their tissues contain rather than what their wood can be made into: resins, barks, aromatic heartwood, alkaloids. Covers both traditional pharmacopoeia and modern drug development.

Worth carrying away

Dragon’s blood — the red resin that bleeds from a cut dragon tree — was valuable enough to be traded into the Roman Empire as a medicine, and has been in continuous commerce for two thousand years. The general point: a resin or bark trade is only sustainable if the harvest doesn’t require killing the tree, and for aromatic heartwoods it usually does.

Section 2.2

Precious timbers, including instrument woods

The high-value timber trade: rosewoods, ebonies, and the specific dense tonewoods that clarinets, oboes and guitar backs have been made from for a century and a half. Includes the regulatory response — CITES listings and the enforcement problem behind them.

The uncomfortable bit

Rosewood has been, by volume, among the most trafficked wild products in the world — ahead of ivory and pangolin. The demand is furniture. This is the section that most clearly reframes tree conservation as a wildlife-crime and supply-chain problem rather than a land-management one.

Section 2.3

Fruit and nut trees

Wild fruit and nut species, including the wild relatives of domesticated crops. Covers both the direct harvest and the genetic value of populations that were never bred.

Worth carrying away

The wild ancestors and relatives of orchard crops are themselves threatened — the wild apple forests of Central Asia being the standing example. Those populations are the genetic reservoir plant breeders will need for disease and climate resistance. Losing them narrows the options for the domesticated crop decades before anyone notices they are gone.

Section 2.4

Ornamental trees

Trees grown for beauty, and horticulture’s genuinely double-edged role: nurseries and botanic gardens as both a driver of wild collection and, for some species, the only reason they still exist.

Worth carrying away

Several species in the book have no wild population at all and survive purely in cultivation — Franklinia being the classic case, unseen in the wild since the early 1800s and alive today only because gardeners kept propagating it. Ex-situ collections are not a consolation prize; for some species they are the entire remaining conservation strategy.

Chapter three

Special tree groups

The taxonomic pass. Seven groups, each with a group-level portrait followed by individual species. This is the chapter that makes the scale of the problem legible, because it shows entire lineages under pressure rather than isolated unlucky species.

Group 1

Conifers

An ancient lineage, now proportionally one of the most threatened major plant groups. Includes relict species with tiny wild ranges and the fire, pathogen and logging pressures on them.

Worth carrying away

Conifers are the counterexample to the assumption that rarity means obscurity. Several are globally famous, widely planted, and still down to a handful of wild stands — a species can be in every arboretum in the temperate world and still be one bad fire season from losing its entire natural population.

Group 2

Magnolias

One of the most-planted ornamental genera on earth, and one of the most threatened in the wild. Wild magnolia diversity sits mostly in Asian and Neotropical montane forest, not in the handful of hybrids that dominate gardens.

The uncomfortable bit

Around half of all magnolia species carry a threatened Red List status. The genus is the sharpest illustration of a theme running through the whole book: familiarity in cultivation tells you nothing about security in the wild, and can actively mask it.

Group 3

Ashes

A genus of ordinary, abundant, unglamorous trees being taken apart by an introduced beetle and an introduced fungus rather than by logging or clearance.

The uncomfortable bit

Emerald ash borer and ash dieback have pushed several North American ash species onto the Red List as critically endangered — species that were, within living memory, common street and forest trees. Pests and pathogens move through protected areas as freely as through anywhere else, which makes this the threat class least amenable to conventional protection.

Group 4

Oaks

A large genus, culturally central in Europe and North America, but with its real diversity centred on Mexico and East Asia. Roughly a third of oak species carry a threatened status.

Worth carrying away

Acorns are recalcitrant — they cannot be dried and frozen, so oaks cannot be conserved in a seed bank. Every oak conservation collection has to be a living collection of growing trees, maintained in perpetuity by an institution. That single seed-physiology fact dictates the entire conservation approach for the genus.

Group 5

Dipterocarps

The family that structures Southeast Asian lowland rainforest and supplied the region’s timber boom. Large, canopy-dominant, slow to mature, and heavily depleted across their range.

Worth carrying away

Dipterocarps mast: whole forests synchronise fruiting at multi-year intervals rather than seeding annually. It is an elegant strategy for swamping seed predators, and a serious problem for restoration — seed is unavailable for years at a stretch, seedlings cannot be stockpiled, and a logged forest has a narrow and unpredictable window to regenerate at all.

Group 6

Palms

A group with an unusually high proportion of species known from a single population or a single valley, concentrated in Madagascar, Southeast Asia and the Neotropics.

Worth carrying away

Many palms cannot resprout from a cut stem — harvesting the growing point kills the individual outright. For species with a few hundred wild plants, that turns a minor local harvest into a direct extinction driver.

Group 7

Sapotaceae

The least famous group in the chapter and the argument for why it’s there. A tropical family of enormous economic importance — the source of shea, chicle and a long list of regional fruits — contributed by a specialist in the family.

Worth carrying away

Much of the family remains under-collected and under-assessed, with species still being described. That is a general point about tree conservation: for a substantial number of tropical trees we do not yet know enough to say whether they are threatened, and “unassessed” is not the same as “fine”.

Who it’s for

It is priced and shaped as a gift book, which undersells who will actually get the most out of it.

Buy it

If you want one reference that connects a named tree to its ecosystem, its trade and its conservation status — or a conservation book you can leave out and have people actually pick up.

Borrow it

If you already read Red List accounts and want the photographs more than the text. The species entries will feel brief.

Skip it

If you want policy, economics, or an argument about how forest loss actually gets reversed. That book exists; this is not it.

Pair it with

The Global Tree Assessment and BGCI’s State of the World’s Trees reporting, which supply the numbers this book sits on top of.
