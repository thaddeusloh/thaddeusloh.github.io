Mountain RescueEpic tales of search and rescue in high-country New Zealand

by Phillip Melchior — mountaineer, LandSAR Wanaka volunteer, and former global managing director of Reuters Media

Eleven callouts in the Southern Alps, told from both ends of the rope: the trip that went wrong, and the volunteers who walked in after it did.

Plotted to scale. One story reaches back to 1948; the rest of the documented callouts sit in the last decade before publication — which is the book's quiet argument about how much has changed, and how little.

The review

The half of the story a rescuer never sees

Most search and rescue books are written from the helicopter. Melchior's isn't, and that is the whole point of it. He came to the material as a Wanaka LandSAR volunteer who had turned out on real callouts, and found that the people he had carried off a mountain knew a story he didn't — the hours or days before the beacon went off, the small decisions that stacked up, the reason the trip needed rescuing at all. So he went and asked them. The result is a book with two protagonists in every chapter: the party in trouble, and the team walking in.

The structure is simple and holds up well. Eleven incidents, each given room to breathe, chosen with former Wanaka Alpine Cliff Rescue leader Gary Dickson from a longlist of eighteen. They are not a greatest-hits reel. Some end with everyone home; some end with a body bag and a family; at least one ends with a rescuer still turning it over years later. Melchior's first rule for himself was not to apportion blame, and he mostly keeps to it — the tone is closer to an aviation incident report than a morality tale, which makes the recurring mistakes land harder than any lecture would.

His journalism shows in the best way. Forty years of newsroom discipline gives the accounts pace and a refusal to over-write the drama, and his standing inside the SAR community gets him detail that reporters covering these operations never see — including the friction between two rescue teams working the same mountain, a story that had gone entirely unreported. Where he is honest about his own presence, as at the Motatapu, the writing tightens noticeably.

Two weaknesses are worth naming. The book is regionally concentrated: this is largely a Wanaka and Aoraki story, and readers wanting national coverage of New Zealand search and rescue should know that going in. And because Melchior likes his subjects and owes several of them his own safety, the analysis stops short more often than a full outsider's account would — you get the sequence of events cleanly, but rarely a hard verdict on the operational calls. That is a fair trade for the access, and most readers will take it.

Read it for the thing it does better than the news coverage ever did: it takes eleven incidents that were each a paragraph in the paper, and gives them back their duration.

Inside the book

The callouts

Six of the eleven chapters are described in published interviews and reviews; those are set out below in date order. Melchior has confirmed which two open and close the book — and the pairing is deliberate.

1948 La Perouse · Aoraki
Opening chapter

Ruth Adams and the seven-day carry

An accomplished climber, badly injured in a fall in the Aoraki/Mt Cook region, and a rescue that took a full week to bring her out. It was run by the guide Mick Bowie — famously spare with words — with a young Edmund Hillary among the party. Everything that a modern callout compresses into a few hours here takes days of manpower, and Melchior uses it to set the baseline for the rest of the book.

Brought out alive
2003 Mt Tasman

The New Year's Day avalanche

One of the most notorious incidents on New Zealand's second-highest peak, on the first day of the year. An avalanche chapter is the hardest kind to write without slipping into fatalism, and it sits early in the book's argument that the mountain does not care how good you are.

Fatal
2005 Mt Tutoko · Fiordland

Lisa Auer and Jonathon Baird

Two climbers — a Wanaka-based Australian and a Scot — stuck high in Fiordland. The rescue itself succeeded; the chapter's scoop is what happened between the rescuers. Wanaka volunteers, personally invested because the climbers were friends, arrived in Invercargill's territory with an attitude that rankled the local team and the police. Melchior reports it plainly, including his own side's part in it, and it is the most institutionally interesting chapter in the book.

Rescued · inter-team friction
— The Breakaway · Bonar Glacier

Three Americans on Mt Aspiring

Melchior's own nomination for the classic case: three keen young climbers with more enthusiasm than experience, caught by a weather change they lacked the background to read, choosing the wrong line down off the end of the Bonar Glacier. One of the three died in the fall; another lost a leg. If the book has a single cautionary chapter, this is it.

One death · one amputation
2012 Motatapu River

Dion Latta in the waterfall

The chapter Melchior was there for, and the one he says marked the team most. Latta's foot jammed in rock at the lip of a waterfall and the force of the water pushed him over the edge, leaving him hanging in freezing whitewater that was too powerful for anyone to work against. An air pocket kept him breathing. It took three hours to free him, and three hours was too long — he later died of the effects of hypothermia. Melchior writes it without embellishment, which is why it is the hardest chapter to read.

Fatal · author present
2012 Bonar Glacier · Mt Aspiring
Closing chapter

Ian Larratt, found by his father

A 27-year-old Canadian, snowblind in foul weather on Mt Aspiring, extremely well equipped but with no intentions filed locally — nobody in New Zealand knew he was on the mountain. His personal locator beacon pushed a position every ten minutes, and his father, watching from overseas, saw that the dot had not moved for five hours and called the police. Melchior pairs it deliberately with the 1948 opener: sixty-four years apart, one rescue slowed by having no technology, one made possible by it, and a survivor who still nearly died of an old-fashioned failure to tell anyone where he was going.

Survived

On the other five chapters. Melchior cut his longlist of eighteen incidents down to eleven purely to hit a 60,000-word limit, but the remaining five stories haven't been itemised in any interview or review I could find. They are in the book; they just aren't in the public record around it.

What runs through it

Four recurring threads

Thread one

The weather is faster than you are

Melchior names underestimating how quickly alpine conditions turn as one of the two commonest errors across the eleven incidents. It shows up in nearly every chapter, usually in people who were otherwise competent.

Thread two

Equipped for the good day only

The other common error: parties carrying enough for the trip as planned, and nothing for the trip as it actually went. Gear for the failure case is what separates a bad night out from a callout.

Thread three

Technology cuts both ways

The 1948-to-2012 arc is the book's spine. A beacon can collapse a seven-day carry into a single flight — but it doesn't replace telling someone where you're going, and the closing chapter is a survivor who nearly proved it.

Thread four

What it costs the volunteers

Melchior is direct about the psychological freight, and about a SAR culture that used to shrug it off with a beer and now talks about it. One team leader speaks to the dead as though they can still hear him, because it makes the job survivable.

Behind the book

Written on a deadline of a different kind

Penguin Random House approached Melchior about the book just as he was starting four months of chemotherapy for amyloidosis, a rare blood disorder diagnosed in 2012, at which point he had been given six to nine months. He took the commission partly to have something to do other than contemplate that. He wrote the first chapter in three days and had a draft inside about two months, delivering the manuscript in December, comfortably ahead of a January deadline. He went into remission and outlived the prognosis by years.

He was also clear-eyed about why the stories should be told at all: LandSAR runs on donations, and a community that doesn't know what its volunteers do can't reasonably be asked to fund them. Proceeds from the book went to LandSAR Wanaka.

Verdict

Who should pick it up

Read it if you tramp or climb in New Zealand and want the case studies rather than the safety pamphlet; if you like disaster reconstruction done with restraint; or if you want to understand what a volunteer rescue organisation actually is.

Skip it if you're after a systematic history of search and rescue in New Zealand, or a technical manual — it is neither, and doesn't pretend to be.

Pairs well with the TV series High Country Rescue, filmed with the same Wanaka team, which covers the operational side the book takes as read.
