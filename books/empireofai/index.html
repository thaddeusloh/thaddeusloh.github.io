The argument

What the whole book is trying to establish.

Karen Hao spent seven years covering artificial intelligence, first for MIT Technology Review and later for the Wall Street Journal. In 2019 she became the first journalist given access inside OpenAI, then a small nonprofit lab. The profile she published afterwards described a gap between the openness the organisation advertised and the secrecy it actually practised. That gap became the seed of the book.

Her thesis is that the leading AI companies are best understood as a new form of empire. Old empires claimed territory, minerals and labour, concentrated the proceeds in a small metropolitan elite, and justified all of it with a story about civilising the world. Hao argues that the AI industry does the same things with different inputs: it claims the world's written and visual output as training data, buys cheap labour from countries in economic crisis, sites enormous power- and water-hungry facilities in communities with little leverage, and defends the arrangement with the promise of a technology that will cure disease and end poverty.

The second half of the argument is about choice. Hao insists that the dominant approach — build ever larger general-purpose models by throwing more compute and more scraped data at them — is one engineering bet among several, not a law of nature. It won because a small group of well-capitalised people believed in it and could afford to make it, and because an arms-race narrative made slowing down feel like surrender. If it is a choice, it can be chosen differently.

Scale as ideology

The scaling bet became a belief system inside OpenAI before it was an empirical result. Once adopted, it justified any amount of resource consumption, because the prize was framed as infinite.

A mission that absorbs criticism

"Benefit all of humanity" is unfalsifiable and enormously useful. It licenses secrecy, commercial aggression and haste, all reframed as responsibility.

An invisible supply chain

Systems marketed as automated rest on a hidden layer of human labour — annotating, ranking, filtering — bought cheaply in Kenya, Venezuela and elsewhere, and kept off the org chart.

Governance that did not hold

OpenAI's nonprofit board was designed to restrain the company if it drifted. In November 2023 it tried, and lost in five days. Hao treats that as the book's proof, not its climax.

How the book is built

A prologue, four parts, eighteen chapters and an epilogue. The spine is roughly chronological, but Hao repeatedly steps out of Silicon Valley to follow the consequences elsewhere.

Prologue opens with the November 2023 board crisis and poses the question the rest of the book answers: how did a nonprofit founded to protect humanity from AI reach a point where its own board decided it could not trust the chief executive?

Part I establishes origins and ideology. Part II covers commercialisation and the labour that made it possible. Part III widens out to ideology wars, planetary resources, regulation and personal harm. Part IV returns to the boardroom, then generalises the pattern into a formula. The Epilogue offers alternatives.

The chronology underneath

  • 2015 OpenAI founded as a nonprofit after a dinner convened by Sam Altman, with Elon Musk among the backers.
  • 2018 Musk departs after a failed bid for control; the charter is published.
  • 2019 Hao embeds with the company. OpenAI creates a capped-profit arm and takes a billion dollars from Microsoft.
  • 2020 GPT-3 and the commercial API. Safety concerns are raised internally and overtaken.
  • 2021 A group of senior researchers leaves to found Anthropic.
  • Nov 2022 ChatGPT is released and becomes the fastest-growing consumer product on record.
  • 2023 GPT-4, a global regulatory push, and in November the five-day crisis that fires and restores Altman.
  • 2024 Ilya Sutskever, Jan Leike and Mira Murati depart; the superalignment team is dissolved; investors press for a full for-profit conversion.
  • May 2025 Empire of AI is published. OpenAI declined to cooperate with it.

Chapter by chapter

Thematic summaries written from the book's reporting, with a note on what each chapter contributes to the argument. Chapter titles are Hao's; the wording of the summaries is not.

Inside the command centre Out at the periphery
Prologue

A Run for the Throne

A close reconstruction of 17 November 2023: the video call on which Sutskever tells Altman the board has removed him, the employee revolt that followed, Microsoft's intervention, and the reinstatement days later. Hao uses the episode — known internally as the Blip — as an opening puzzle rather than a finale, and rewinds to explain it.

Contribution: frames the whole book as an inquiry into how power actually sits inside the company.

Part I

Origins and ideology

Where OpenAI came from, what its founders believed, and why "bigger" became the answer to every question.

1

Divine Right

Founding

The 2015 dinner, the rivalry with Google DeepMind, Musk's conviction that AI posed an existential threat, and his argument with Larry Page about whether machines surpassing humans would even be a problem. Hao traces the founding belief that the future of humanity should be steered by a small circle of people confident they are the right ones to steer it.

Contribution: the aristocratic premise the empire metaphor rests on.

2

A Civilizing Mission

Mission

The early nonprofit years: Greg Brockman as operator, Ilya Sutskever as the technical conscience, and a mission statement broad enough to justify almost anything. Hao draws the explicit parallel to the imperial civilising mission — a stated intention to benefit everyone that also happens to authorise the actor to do whatever it judges necessary.

Contribution: names the rhetorical device the rest of the book keeps finding.

3

Nerve Center

Reporting

Hao's own three days inside OpenAI in 2019. She describes evasive answers from leadership, restricted access to staff, and an organisation whose internal reality had already diverged from its public self-description. Her resulting profile was not welcomed.

Contribution: establishes her sourcing and the secrecy theme first-hand.

4

Dreams of Modernity

History

A compressed history of AI from the coining of the term in 1956 through the symbolic and connectionist schools to the deep learning breakthrough. The point is comparative: the field has repeatedly changed its mind about what intelligence is and how to build it, so today's consensus should not be mistaken for a destination. AGI, meanwhile, has never been given a usable definition.

Contribution: the "it could have been otherwise" foundation for the entire critique.

5

Scale of Ambition

Technology

The transformer architecture, the discovery that performance improved predictably with more compute and data, and the internal decision to bet everything on that curve. The appetite quickly outran the supply of good material, so the models were fed progressively lower-quality data — and the compute bill made outside capital unavoidable.

Contribution: shows how a technical result hardened into a strategy that required commercialisation.

Part II

Commerce and hidden labour

The turn to product, the research culture it displaced, and the workers who made the products safe enough to ship.

6

Ascension

Restructure

The creation of the capped-profit entity, Altman's move to full-time chief executive, the Microsoft partnership, and the dedicated supercomputer. Hao details how a structure meant to keep investors subordinate to the mission instead made the mission dependent on investors.

Contribution: the governance compromise that everything later turns on.

7

Science in Captivity

Research

What happens to AI research when nearly all of it is funded by the companies being studied. The central case is Timnit Gebru's firing from Google after co-authoring a paper on the environmental costs and social risks of large language models. Hao pairs it with OpenAI's own internal split, when researchers worried about safety and control left to found Anthropic.

Contribution: explains why independent scrutiny of these systems is structurally scarce.

8

Dawn of Commerce

Product

GPT-3's release and the developer API in 2020, shipped over objections from the safety team. To sell a model trained on the open internet, the company needed it to stop producing the internet's worst material — which created the requirement for reinforcement learning from human feedback and for a content filter, and therefore for people to build both.

Contribution: the causal link between commercial ambition and outsourced human suffering.

9

Disaster Capitalism

Kenya · Venezuela

Hao reports from Nairobi, where workers contracted through an outsourcing firm read and categorised descriptions of extreme violence and sexual abuse so the filter could learn to recognise them, for a few dollars an hour and without meaningful psychological support. In Venezuela, economic collapse produced a pool of educated, desperate annotators paid by the task. Her argument is that this is not incidental: the industry systematically seeks out places where crisis has made labour cheap.

Contribution: the book's most concrete evidence for extraction, and its most disturbing chapter.

Part III

Gods, resources and rules

The ideological civil war, the physical footprint, the capture of regulation, and one private story of unaccountable power.

10

Gods and Demons

Ideology

The doomers, who fear AI will extinguish humanity, against the boomers, who believe it will save humanity and that delay is the real risk. Hao's reading is that the two camps are less opposed than they appear: both accept that AGI is imminent and enormously powerful, both conclude that they personally must be the ones building it, and both treat present-day harms as a rounding error.

Contribution: dissolves the industry's favourite debate by showing what both sides agree on.

11

Apex

ChatGPT

The November 2022 launch, intended as a modest research preview, and the growth that followed. Then GPT-4, Microsoft's search integration, Google's emergency response, and an industry-wide race in which shipping speed replaced caution as the operative virtue.

Contribution: the moment the empire acquires its economy.

12

Plundered Earth

Chile · Uruguay · US

The physical substrate: data centres drawing electricity and fresh water, and the mining that supplies the hardware. Hao reports from Chile, where residents of Cerrillos organised against a proposed facility during a decade-long drought, from Uruguay, and from Arizona and Iowa. She connects these to lithium and copper extraction in the Atacama, and to communities that were consulted late or not at all.

Contribution: makes the cloud physical, and local. See the correction note under Criticism.

13

The Two Prophets

Regulation

Two competing warnings compete for the attention of lawmakers: the existential-risk warning, carried by eminent figures and well funded, and the present-harm warning from researchers, artists and affected workers. Hao follows Altman's 2023 Senate testimony and international tour and argues that the industry embraced the first framing precisely because rules written against a speculative future cost far less than rules written against current practice.

Contribution: explains why loud calls for regulation produced so little of it.

14

Deliverance

Accountability

Hao reports the allegations of abuse made publicly by Annie Altman, Sam Altman's sister, along with her account of years of financial precarity. The chapter is not presented as a verdict. It is placed as an argument about asymmetry: what recourse exists against someone with money, reputation and institutional backing — and the answer she finds is much the same as the one available to a Kenyan annotator or a Chilean water activist.

Contribution: the book's most contested chapter, and its thematic hinge on accountability.

Part IV

The crisis and the formula

Back to the boardroom, then out to the general pattern.

15

The Gambit

Build-up

How trust broke down. Sutskever, chief technology officer Mira Murati and board member Helen Toner accumulated separate concerns about Altman's candour and his habit of telling different people different things. A research paper by Toner and Altman's reaction to it, disputes over the venture fund's ownership, and the board's growing sense that it could not verify what it was told.

Contribution: the evidentiary basis for the firing, assembled from internal accounts.

16

Cloak-and-Dagger

The Blip

The five days in full: the removal, the board's refusal to explain itself publicly, the mass employee letter threatening to leave for Microsoft, Sutskever's reversal, and Altman's return with a reconstituted board. Hao's reading is that the nonprofit's control was notional from the moment the company's value depended on the loyalty of its staff and the money of its partner.

Contribution: the practical demonstration that the safeguard could not fire.

17

Reckoning

Aftermath

The consolidation that followed: safety-focused staff departing, the superalignment team dissolved, Murati's exit, the reporting on restrictive exit agreements tied to vested equity, and the push toward a conventional for-profit structure with a deadline attached. Meanwhile, compute ambitions grew by an order of magnitude.

Contribution: shows the crisis strengthening exactly what it was meant to check.

18

A Formula for Empire

Synthesis

Hao sets out the pattern explicitly: seize a resource and declare the seizure normal; source labour where bargaining power is weakest; keep the workings secret while claiming openness; justify it all with a civilising promise; and when challenged, invoke a rival empire — in this case China — whose victory would be worse. She argues the formula now travels beyond OpenAI to the whole frontier industry.

Contribution: generalises the case from one company to a model.

Epilogue

How the Empire Falls

The alternatives. Hao profiles Te Hiku Media, a Māori organisation that built speech recognition for te reo Māori using community-contributed data under a licence that keeps control with the community — small, task-specific, consented, and effective. She points to the Distributed AI Research Institute founded by Timnit Gebru, to unionising annotators, to artists litigating over training data, and to the Chilean activists who stopped a data centre. Empires do not usually fall to a single blow; they fall when enough people stop believing the story that justifies them.

Contribution: converts the critique into a programme, and answers the charge that the book only complains.

Events the book is built around

  • The founding dinner and the Musk split. OpenAI began as a response to Google's acquisition of DeepMind. Musk left in 2018 after an unsuccessful attempt to take control, taking his funding commitment with him and creating the capital pressure that led to the Microsoft deal.
  • The Anthropic divorce. In 2021 a group of senior OpenAI researchers, concerned about safety and about the direction of the company, left to start a competitor — a split Hao treats as the first sign the governance structure could not resolve disagreement.
  • The Gebru firing. Google's dismissal of Timnit Gebru after a paper on the risks and costs of large language models is Hao's exhibit for how narrowly the field polices criticism of its own methods.
  • The content-moderation contract. Building a filter that would keep ChatGPT shippable required people to read the material the filter had to catch. Several of the Kenyan workers Hao interviewed described lasting psychological damage.
  • The Cerrillos campaign. Residents outside Santiago organised against a planned data centre during Chile's prolonged drought, and delayed it — one of the book's clearest examples of successful resistance.
  • The Blip. The board fired Altman for not being consistently candid, could not or would not explain publicly, faced near-total employee opposition, and folded. A subsequent review cleared him.
  • The exits of 2024. Sutskever, Jan Leike and Mira Murati left; the superalignment effort was wound down; investors pressed for a full for-profit conversion.

Worth knowing

The details that stick, and that make the book more than a corporate history.

  • The title is a deliberate historical claim, not a metaphor of convenience. Hao has said she went to Kenya, Chile and Uruguay precisely because you cannot describe an empire from inside its capital. The structure of the book enacts the argument.
  • She was the first journalist to profile OpenAI from the inside — and the access was never repeated. The company declined to participate in the book, which she reported instead through former staff, documents and internal messages.
  • AGI has no agreed definition inside the companies pursuing it. Hao presses this point repeatedly: an undefined goal cannot be reached, missed, or measured, which makes it a remarkably durable justification.
  • Doomers and boomers are closer than they look. Both accept that a world-transforming system is coming soon and that they should be the ones to build it. The disagreement is about tone, not authority.
  • "Automated" systems are staffed. The reason a chatbot will not describe an atrocity is that someone was paid to read thousands of descriptions of atrocities. That labour is contracted out, geographically distant, and absent from every product launch.
  • Existential-risk lobbying was commercially convenient. Rules aimed at a hypothetical future superintelligence impose almost no cost on current products. Rules about training data, labour conditions or water use would.
  • The people Hao expected to be powerless were the ones who fought. Kenyan annotators organised and went to the international press; Chilean activists blocked a data centre; artists sued. She has described this as the most hopeful part of the reporting.
  • Te Hiku Media is the counter-example she leaves you with. A Māori-led speech recognition project built on community-donated data, governed by a licence that keeps the data under community control — small, specific, consented, and good at its job.

A technology that needs an empire to exist is telling you something about the technology.

The takeaway

If you read nothing else, this is what the book wants you to leave with.

  1. The costs are present, not future. The debate about whether AI will one day threaten humanity has crowded out an accounting of what it is already consuming: labour, water, power, minerals, and the unconsented work of everyone whose writing and images became training data.
  2. Scale is a business strategy wearing the costume of a scientific necessity. The bet on ever-larger general models was made by people who could afford to make it. Smaller, task-specific, consent-based systems remain viable and in some settings work better.
  3. Mission language is a governance risk. An aim broad enough to justify anything will eventually be used to justify anything. OpenAI's structure was explicitly designed to hold the mission above profit, and it did not survive contact with a five-day test.
  4. Concentration is the actual danger. Hao's worry is less that a machine becomes uncontrollable than that a handful of firms end up controlling the infrastructure of knowledge work, with no democratic mandate and no meaningful external audit.
  5. None of this is settled. The book ends on agency rather than despair: annotators organised, communities blocked facilities, artists went to court. Empires persist because their story holds, and stories can stop holding.

If you work with this technology

The book is most useful read as a due-diligence checklist rather than a verdict. The questions it implies are practical ones: where did the training data come from and under what permission; who did the annotation and on what terms; what does a given deployment actually cost in energy and water; is a large general model the right instrument for this task or just the available one; and who can audit the answer if it turns out to be wrong.

Criticism and counterpoints

Reading the book well means knowing where it has been challenged. Included so you can weigh the argument rather than absorb it.

A corrected water figure

In late 2025, after fact-checking by the writer Andy Masley and others, Hao acknowledged that the published text overstated the water a proposed Chilean data centre would use relative to the surrounding community by roughly a factor of a thousand, traced to a unit-conversion error in a government document she relied on. She issued a correction. Critics argue the mistake was load-bearing for one of the book's most-quoted passages; supporters note she corrected it openly and that the wider siting and drought reporting stands. Treat specific environmental magnitudes in the book as needing a second source.

The empire frame does work — and also flattens

Reviewers who admire the reporting have questioned the analogy. Historical empires held sovereign territory and deployed state violence; AI firms sign commercial contracts in jurisdictions that can, at least in principle, refuse them. The comparison illuminates the flow of resources and the rhetoric of benevolence, but it can compress very different relationships — a Nairobi outsourcing contract, a Chilean planning permission, a scraped webpage — into a single moral category.

Benefits are given little room

The book concentrates on costs. Readers looking for a serious accounting of what these systems demonstrably do well — in software engineering, translation, accessibility, drug discovery, or for people without access to expertise — will find that side thinly argued. Hao's reply, in interviews, is that the benefits already have well-funded advocates and that her job was the missing half of the ledger. Whether that is a fair division of labour for a book of this influence is a reasonable thing to disagree about.

The Altman family chapter

Chapter 14 reports allegations by Altman's sister that he and other family members have denied. Some reviewers found it essential to the book's argument about accountability; others considered it a departure from the institutional critique and a shift into personal territory that the empire thesis does not require. Read it aware that it is contested and that it was not resolved by the reporting.

Altman and OpenAI's position

OpenAI declined to cooperate with the book and Altman has publicly disputed its characterisation of him and of the company. Their broad counter-argument is that safety, research openness and broad benefit remain genuine priorities, that the commercial structure is what funds the research, and that the technology's upside is being understated. No detailed rebuttal of the book's specific reporting has been published.

Reception, for context

The book was a New York Times bestseller and won the National Book Critics Circle Award for Nonfiction, the Los Angeles Times Book Prize and the New York Public Library's Helen Bernstein Award, among others. It was named a book of the year by the Financial Times, Smithsonian, Scientific American and others. The critical consensus praises the reporting depth; the recurring reservation is that the framing is advocacy as much as journalism.
