Reader's guide — Parmy Olson — St. Martin's Press / Pan Macmillan, September 2024

Supremacy

AI, ChatGPT and the Race That Will Change the World

Two founders set out to build artificial general intelligence for the benefit of humanity. Both needed more compute than any independent organisation could buy. Both ended up working for a trillion-dollar company, and the race between them is now a proxy war between Google and Microsoft.

The argument

What the book is trying to establish, and why it is structured as a double portrait rather than a single story.

Parmy Olson is a Bloomberg Opinion columnist who has covered AI and technology regulation for years, previously at the Wall Street Journal and Forbes. At the Journal she was the first reporter to reveal that Google's leading AI lab had secretly tried to break away from its parent company in order to control what it built. That story is the seed of this book.

Her thesis has three parts. First, that the modern AI race is best explained through two men with genuinely idealistic motives: Demis Hassabis, who wanted to solve intelligence so that intelligence could solve science, and Sam Altman, who wanted to generate so much abundance that scarcity would stop mattering. Neither set out to build a product business.

Second, that both were defeated by arithmetic. Training frontier models costs more than any independent lab can raise, and the only organisations with that much money and that much compute are a handful of technology giants. So Hassabis sold DeepMind to Google and Altman bound OpenAI to Microsoft — in both cases believing the mission could be insulated from the money. Olson's reporting shows, repeatedly, that it could not. The ethics board attached to the Google acquisition never became a meaningful check. Hassabis's attempt to win back independence failed. OpenAI's nonprofit governance collapsed in five days when it tried to exercise its authority.

Third, and this is where she parts company with most AI books, she argues that the loud debate about whether AI will one day destroy humanity is itself part of the problem. Existential risk is a flattering story for the people telling it — it implies their technology is world-shaping — and it is cheap to regulate against. Meanwhile the actual damage being done now is mundane and commercial: biased systems sold into hiring, lending, education and healthcare; a flood of synthetic content; work quietly deskilled; and two companies acquiring a degree of influence over knowledge itself that no democratic body authorised.

The danger isn't that the machines get out of control. It's that two companies won't.

The two men

The book only works because the protagonists are genuinely different. Same destination, opposite reasons for wanting to get there — and opposite ways of losing control of it.

Demis Hassabis

DeepMind, founded 2010 — the scientist

Background
Chess prodigy, then a teenage games designer, then a cognitive neuroscience PhD on memory and imagination. Came to AI through wanting to understand how minds work.
The utopia
Solve intelligence, then use it to solve everything else — protein folding, disease, fusion, the structure of the universe. AI as the ultimate scientific instrument.
The bargain
Sold to Google in 2014 for the server farms, negotiating an ethics board and, reportedly, restrictions on military use as conditions.
How it went wrong
Slow absorption. The safeguards were never load-bearing, an attempt to regain independence failed, and in 2023 the lab was merged into Google's broader AI effort and pointed at a product race it had not chosen.

Sam Altman

OpenAI, founded 2015 — the operator

Background
Founded a location-sharing startup that failed, then ran Y Combinator. Not a researcher. A capital allocator, recruiter and extraordinarily effective teller of stories about the future.
The utopia
Build something so productive that abundance replaces scarcity and the returns can be distributed. AI as an economic engine rather than a telescope.
The bargain
Created a capped-profit arm under the nonprofit and took a billion dollars from Microsoft in 2019, keeping the mission language and the governance structure intact on paper.
How it went wrong
Rapid and public. The structure designed to keep the mission above profit was tested in November 2023, when the board removed him. It lost within a week, and the board was replaced.

Olson's point in running them side by side is that the outcome was the same despite the differences. One founder was a scientist who wanted to go slowly, one was an operator who wanted to ship. One was absorbed quietly over nine years, one was checked publicly over five days. Both ended up inside a Big Tech company, racing the other. If two very different people with two very different philosophies arrive at the same place, the explanation is probably structural rather than personal — which is the case she is making.

How the book is built

Four parts, sixteen short chapters, moving roughly chronologically and alternating between the two labs. The part titles carry the argument on their own.

  • Part One — The Dream (chapters 1–6). Origins. Who these two men were before any of this, what they actually wanted, and how a mission statement became the most valuable asset either organisation had.
  • Part Two — The Leviathans (chapters 7–9). Capture. Google buys DeepMind, the honeymoon, and then the slow realisation that the safeguards negotiated on the way in were decorative.
  • Part Three — The Bills (chapters 10–12). The cost. Why scale made independence impossible, how both labs ended up bound to a giant, and how the researchers warning about present-day harm got sidelined.
  • Part Four — The Race (chapters 13–16). The collision. ChatGPT, the doom discourse, the boardroom crisis, and what it means to live downstream of two companies that control the technology.

It is a business and power book, not a technical one. There is very little explanation of how transformers or training actually work, and that is deliberate — Olson is writing about the people, the money and the governance. If you want the technology explained, this is the wrong book and that is not a flaw.

Chapter by chapter

Chapter titles are Olson's. The summaries are thematic — what each chapter covers and what it contributes — written from the book's reporting rather than its text.

Part One — The Dream

Chapters 1–6 · who they were and what they wanted

The most human stretch of the book, and the part that makes the later capture land.

1

High School Hero

Altman

Altman's upbringing in St. Louis and the formative moment when he stood up in front of his school over its treatment of gay students. Olson uses it to establish the thing that recurs throughout his career: an unusual willingness to put himself at the centre of a moral argument, combined with a talent for getting a room to follow him. It is a sympathetic opening, and it is doing real work — the rest of the book depends on you believing the idealism was once sincere.

Contributes: Altman as a persuader before he was ever a technologist.

2

Winning, Winning, Winning

Hassabis

Hassabis's childhood as a chess prodigy in north London, his years designing video games as a teenager, and his record at multi-discipline games competitions. Games are not incidental colour here. They are how he learned to think about intelligence as something general — a capacity that transfers across domains rather than a set of tricks — and they become the proving ground for DeepMind's earliest results.

Contributes: the intellectual origin of "solve intelligence, then solve everything else."

3

Save the Humans

Ideology

How the save-the-world framing took hold. The existential-risk community, Elon Musk's alarm about what would happen if the wrong people built AGI first, and the peculiar logic it produces: if the technology is dangerous, the responsible thing is to build it yourself, quickly, before someone worse does. Olson treats this as sincerely held and self-serving at the same time, which is the tone she keeps for the rest of the book.

Contributes: the belief system that justified racing.

4

A Better Brain

DeepMind

Hassabis's turn to cognitive neuroscience and the founding of DeepMind in 2010 with Shane Legg and Mustafa Suleyman. The bet was that studying how biological memory and imagination work would show how to build general intelligence in software. Early on almost nobody took it seriously — a London startup claiming it would solve intelligence — and the chapter covers the years of persuading anyone to fund it.

Contributes: DeepMind as a research institute that happened to be a company.

5

For Utopia, For Money

Capital

The money arrives, and with it the contradiction in the title. Peter Thiel, Founders Fund and Musk among the early backers. Investors who want returns are asked to fund an organisation that says it is building something for all humanity. Both sides tell themselves the tension is manageable. Olson's argument is that this is the moment the ending becomes predictable — not the acquisition years later.

Contributes: the original compromise, made long before anyone sold anything.

6

The Mission

OpenAI

OpenAI is founded in December 2015 with a billion dollars in pledges, explicitly as a counterweight to Google's dominance and to DeepMind in particular. Olson is interested in what the mission statement actually does as an object: it recruits researchers who would never join a normal company, it attracts capital, it deflects criticism, and because it is unfalsifiable it can accommodate almost any subsequent decision.

Contributes: mission as infrastructure, not as sentiment.

Part Two — The Leviathans

Chapters 7–9 · absorbed by the giants

The strongest reporting in the book, and the section that drew the most attention when it was published.

7

Playing Games

DeepMind · Google

Two games at once. DeepMind's systems learn to play Atari titles from raw pixels and then, in March 2016, AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol — the moment AI became globally visible for the first time. And the corporate game: Google acquires DeepMind in January 2014, reportedly for around $650 million, after competing interest from Facebook and pressure from Musk. Hassabis negotiates conditions, including an ethics board and reported limits on military and surveillance use.

Contributes: the triumph and the sale, side by side, because they were the same event.

8

Everything Is Awesome

Inside Google

The honeymoon, and the gap between the corporate cheerfulness and what was actually happening. DeepMind pushes into healthcare and runs into a serious controversy over patient data obtained from an NHS trust. AlphaFold delivers genuine scientific value. Meanwhile the lab is expensive, its independence is eroding by increments, and the ethics board that was a condition of the sale is doing nothing in particular.

Contributes: capture happens through a hundred small decisions, none of which feels like the decisive one.

9

The Goliath Paradox

The core chapter

The book's central idea, stated plainly. To build AI at the scale required to do any of the good things you promised, you need resources only a monopoly possesses. Accepting them means the monopoly decides what you build. Olson lays out Hassabis's secret, years-long effort to restructure DeepMind out from under Google's control — reporting she broke at the Wall Street Journal — and its failure. A company that has spent hundreds of millions on an asset does not hand it back.

Contributes: names the trap. If you read one chapter, this is it.

Part Three — The Bills

Chapters 10–12 · what it cost and who paid

Where the book shifts from narrative to argument.

10

Size Matters

Scale

The scaling era. Bigger models trained on more data with more compute keep getting better, which converts AI research from a question of ideas into a question of budget. Olson traces what this does to the field: it concentrates capability in whoever can afford the bill, it makes independent verification impossible because nobody outside can reproduce the runs, and it forces every serious lab to find a corporate patron.

Contributes: the economic mechanism behind everything else.

11

Bound to Big Tech

The bargains

Both bargains fully consummated. Microsoft's billion-dollar investment in 2019 and the capped-profit restructure that made it possible, with Microsoft as exclusive cloud provider. On the other side, DeepMind folded together with Google Brain in 2023 into a single unit under commercial pressure. Two labs founded to be independent of corporate interests are now the AI divisions of the two companies they were founded to counterbalance.

Contributes: the symmetry that gives the book its structure.

12

Myth Busters

Ethics

The researchers who tried to raise present-tense problems — bias, opacity, environmental cost, the limits of what these systems actually do — and what happened to them. Timnit Gebru's dismissal from Google is the central case. Olson also goes after inflated commercial claims: companies that described ordinary software as AI, and results that did not survive scrutiny. Her point is that the field polices criticism of its methods more effectively than it polices its products.

Contributes: the harms that were visible all along and got argued out of the room.

Part Four — The Race

Chapters 13–16 · the collision and the aftermath

The events most readers already know, retold with the governance context the earlier parts built.

13

Hello, ChatGPT

November 2022

The launch, the fastest consumer adoption on record, and the institutional panic that followed. Google declares an internal emergency over a technology built on a paper its own researchers published. Bard is rushed out and fumbles its first public demonstration. Microsoft puts the model into Bing with results that get strange fast. Olson's framing is that the product decision, not the model, was the event.

Contributes: the moment the rivalry stopped being private.

14

A Vague Sense of Doom

The argument chapter

2023's wave of existential warnings — the open letter calling for a pause, the one-sentence extinction statement, Geoffrey Hinton leaving Google to speak freely, Altman's Senate testimony and world tour. Olson's reading is sceptical and it is the most contestable thing in the book: she argues that apocalyptic framing was commercially useful, since it implied enormous capability, attracted talent and capital, and pointed regulators at a hypothetical future rather than at current products. Doom, in her account, was partly a marketing position.

Contributes: the claim that will most divide readers, and the one worth arguing with.

15

Checkmate

November 2023

The board crisis. Altman is removed by a board with formal authority to do exactly that, nearly every employee threatens to leave for Microsoft, and he is back within days with a new board. Olson reads it as the decisive demonstration of where power actually sits: not with the governance structure, but with the capital, the compute and the staff. The chess title is not accidental, given who the other protagonist is.

Contributes: the proof that the safeguards were never going to hold.

16

In the Shadow of Monopolies

Conclusion

The closing argument. Two companies now sit astride the technology, with no obligation to disclose what their systems are trained on or how they behave, and independent researchers cannot audit either. Olson catalogues the consequences already arriving — synthetic content at scale, automated decisions in hiring and lending, the deskilling of creative and knowledge work — and argues for the unglamorous remedies: transparency requirements on training data, competition enforcement, and treating this as a market-structure problem rather than a science-fiction one.

Contributes: converts the narrative into a policy position.

The events the book turns on

  • The ethics board that never worked. The most-cited detail in the book. Hassabis negotiated an ethics board as a condition of selling to Google, and it never became a functioning constraint. Olson treats it as the template for every governance promise that followed.
  • The failed spin-out. Olson's own scoop, first reported at the Wall Street Journal: DeepMind spent years quietly trying to restructure itself out from under Google's control so it could govern what it built. Google declined. This is the single best illustration of the Goliath paradox.
  • Musk's interventions. Musk's attempts to influence where DeepMind ended up, his role in funding and founding OpenAI, and his departure in 2018 after failing to take control — which removed his funding and made the Microsoft deal necessary.
  • The NHS data controversy. DeepMind's health work obtained patient records from a UK hospital trust in an arrangement later found to have fallen short on data protection. An early, concrete example of good intentions colliding with a real institution.
  • Timnit Gebru's firing. Google dismisses a leading AI ethics researcher after a paper on the risks and costs of large language models. Olson's exhibit for how narrowly the industry tolerates internal criticism of its methods.
  • The code red. Google's emergency response to ChatGPT, and the fumbled launch of Bard — a company with better technology losing the market because it had more to lose by shipping.
  • The Blip. The five-day crisis at OpenAI, which Olson reads not as a drama about one man but as a live test of whether nonprofit control over a commercial AI lab means anything. It did not.

Worth knowing

The details that stick, and the ones that change how you read the rest.

  • The title has two meanings and you are meant to notice. "Supremacy" is what the technology promises — systems that surpass us. It is also what the two companies have achieved over the market. Olson's book is far more interested in the second.
  • Hassabis is the more sympathetic figure and the book knows it. He is a working scientist with real results behind him, and his capture reads as a tragedy. Altman is drawn as a brilliant operator whose relationship to the mission is harder to pin down. Several reviewers have noted the asymmetry; whether it is fair reporting or an authorial preference is a reasonable thing to debate.
  • The two utopias are genuinely different and rarely distinguished. Hassabis wants AGI as an instrument for understanding the universe. Altman wants it as an engine for ending scarcity. One is epistemic, one is economic. They imply completely different priorities, and the fact that both got absorbed into the same product race is the point.
  • Neither founder ran out of idealism. They ran out of money. Olson is careful about this. The mission did not get abandoned in a moment of cynicism; it got outbid by the cost of a training run. That is a more uncomfortable conclusion than a story about greedy founders.
  • OpenAI was founded to stop Google, and ended up making Google unstoppable in a different way. The counterweight to one monopoly turned out to require another. Any theory of AI governance that relies on competition between giants has to reckon with that.
  • Olson broke the spin-out story before anyone knew there would be a book. The reporting predates ChatGPT, which is part of why the book does not read like it was reverse-engineered from the 2023 news cycle.
  • Her scepticism about doom-talk puts her in a minority among AI authors. Most books in this space either take existential risk seriously or ignore it. Olson takes it on as a commercial phenomenon to be explained, which is unusual and is the argument most likely to start a fight at a book club.
  • It won the FT prize the same season several rival AI books appeared. A useful signal about how the business establishment, rather than the technology press, chose to understand this moment.

The takeaway

What the book wants you to leave holding.

  1. Compute costs decided everything. The single fact that explains the shape of the industry is that frontier AI is too expensive for anyone but a monopoly to fund. Every governance structure, mission statement and ethics board in the story was eventually overruled by that arithmetic. If you want a different outcome, you have to change the economics, not the promises.
  2. Governance safeguards fail exactly when they are needed. An ethics board attached to an acquisition, a nonprofit board with the power to remove a chief executive — both existed, both were tested, both lost. A safeguard that only works while nobody objects to it is not a safeguard. Worth carrying into any discussion of AI oversight inside your own organisation.
  3. The people in charge were sincere, and it did not help. Olson's most useful move is refusing the villain story. Hassabis and Altman both meant it. That makes the outcome more alarming rather than less, because it means good intentions at the top are not a control.
  4. Doom talk and accountability are in tension. Arguing about whether AI might end humanity is emotionally compelling, flattering to the industry, and cheap to legislate against. Arguing about training data provenance, model auditing and market concentration is boring and expensive. The second list is where the leverage is.
  5. This is a market-structure problem wearing a science-fiction costume. The question that matters is not whether the systems become uncontrollable but who controls them, on what terms, with what disclosure, and answerable to whom. Right now the answer is: two companies, on their own terms, with almost no disclosure, answerable to shareholders.

Criticism, and how it reads now

Published September 2024. Two years is a long time in this industry, so some of the frame has moved — and some of it has been vindicated.

The two-horse race frame has aged the fastest

The book is structured as OpenAI versus DeepMind, Microsoft versus Google. That was defensible in 2024 and is harder to sustain now. Anthropic has become a frontier competitor of comparable standing, Meta's open-weight releases changed the market's shape, xAI exists, and Chinese labs have repeatedly shown that the capability frontier is not a two-company preserve. The concentration argument survives; the specific duopoly framing has loosened.

Altman gets a thinner portrait than Hassabis

A common reviewer complaint. Hassabis is rendered with real depth as a scientist; Altman appears more as a strategist and communicator, with less interiority. Related: OpenAI's technical contributors — Ilya Sutskever, Alec Radford, Greg Brockman and others — are comparatively underweighted, which slightly distorts the picture of where OpenAI's results actually came from. Karen Hao's Empire of AI is much stronger on Altman specifically.

Light on the technology

If you want to understand what a transformer does, why scaling worked, or how training actually proceeds, this book will not tell you. That is a deliberate scope choice rather than an error, but it means Supremacy is a poor standalone introduction and a good second or third book on the subject.

The doom scepticism cuts both ways

Olson argues that apocalyptic framing served commercial interests. Critics respond that plenty of people warning about catastrophic risk have no commercial stake, that the argument does not become wrong because someone benefits from it, and that dismissing the concern as marketing is itself a rhetorical move. A fair reading is that she has identified a real incentive without settling the underlying question — and she mostly does not claim to.

The great-man frame

The book explains the industry through two individuals, which makes it readable and can overstate personal agency. Olson partly defends against this herself — her conclusion is that structure beat both men — but the narrative machinery still keeps two founders at the centre of forces much larger than either. Hao's book makes the opposite choice and reaches a similar destination, which is instructive about how much the framing actually matters.

What has been vindicated

Quite a lot. Independent labs have continued to consolidate around a handful of compute providers. The capital requirements have increased by an order of magnitude rather than easing. Governance structures have continued to give way under commercial pressure, including OpenAI's own further restructuring. And the present-harm agenda Olson argued was being crowded out — training data provenance, auditing, market concentration — is now the substance of most actual regulation and litigation, rather than the existential framing that dominated the 2023 discourse.

Where it sits on the shelf

Empire of AI

Karen Hao, 2025

The complement. Olson stays in the boardroom and explains the power structure; Hao goes to Nairobi and Santiago and shows what the structure extracts. Read together they cover the centre and the periphery of the same system.

Superintelligence

Nick Bostrom, 2014

The worldview Olson is sceptical of, argued at full strength by its most rigorous proponent. Useful as the steelman for the position chapter 14 attacks — and it was circulating among the people who founded OpenAI.

Genius Makers

Cade Metz, 2021

The prequel. Covers the deep learning researchers and the talent auctions of 2012–2019 in more technical detail, and explains how the people in Olson's book acquired their leverage in the first place.
