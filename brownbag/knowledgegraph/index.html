A brown bag on how machines keep what they know

One world, two ways to remember it.

Here are twelve things from Maya's neighbourhood. A vector database keeps them in a pile, with similar things sitting close together. A knowledge graph keeps them on a map, with a line drawn between anything that is actually connected. Same twelve things. Press a button and watch them rearrange.

What each one actually holds

The pile knows what things are like. The map knows what things did to each other.

That one sentence is the whole difference. Everything below is just detail.

The pile

a vector database, sometimes called a knowledge base

Imagine a room where you throw in a note for everything you know. As each note lands, it drifts towards other notes that mean something similar. Notes about dogs end up near notes about cats. Nobody sorted them — they sorted themselves by meaning.

To use it, you say what you're looking for and it hands you the twenty nearest notes. It never really understood them. It just knows what sits close to what.

Good at
Find me something like this. Even if I used different words. Even if I spelled it wrong.
Bad at
Anything that needs following a chain. It can hand you two notes that connect, but it can't join them up for you.
Cost to build
Low. Point it at your documents and it mostly builds itself.

At work

This is your policy documents, fund factsheets, product wikis and past advisory notes, chunked and embedded. It gets you a decent search assistant in weeks, and it will always be part of the answer — just rarely all of it.

The map

a knowledge graph

Now imagine a giant pinboard. Every thing you know gets one pin with its name on it: Maya, Coco, Chess Club, Mr. Tan. Then you run a string between two pins whenever something is true about both, and you write on the string what it is: owns, best friend, runs.

Nothing is fuzzy here. Either the string is there or it isn't. And because the strings join up, you can start at one pin and walk to a pin three strings away — which is where the interesting answers live.

Good at
How is this connected to that? Who else? What changes if this one moves?
Bad at
Vague questions. If you don't know which pin to start from, the map can't help you find it.
Cost to build
Real. Somebody has to decide what counts as a pin, and keep the strings honest.

At work

Clients, households, RMs, holdings, products, issuers, sectors, mandates, suitability rules — and the relationships between them. Most of this already exists in your systems as foreign keys. A graph is mainly a decision to treat those joins as first-class, queryable things.

Doing it for real

How you'd actually build one.

The game above skipped the hard parts. Here they are, in the order they bite.

  1. Decide what counts as a pin

    Pick five to ten kinds of thing and the handful of links between them. Write it down as a schema. The urge to model everything is the single most common way these projects die before they ship anything.

  2. Pull the triples out

    Two sources, two methods. Tables, CRM and core systems already have the relationships as joins, so you map them with rules. Documents don't, so you run a language model over them with your schema in the prompt and ask for triples back. Sample the output and have a human check it, because extraction quality sets the ceiling on everything downstream.

  3. Squash the duplicates

    "M. Loh", "Maya Loh" and "Maya L." have to become one pin, or your graph quietly lies to you. This step is called entity resolution and it will take longer than you budgeted. Give it a real owner.

  4. Put it somewhere that can walk

    A graph database (Neo4j, Neptune, TigerGraph) or a triple store. You can also fake it with node and edge tables in the warehouse you already have — worth doing first if you want to prove the value before buying anything.

  5. Add embeddings to the pins

    This is the bit people forget. Give every pin a vector of its name and description, so a messy human question can find the right starting pin. Your graph needs a doorway, and the pile is the doorway.

  6. Keep the receipts

    Every string remembers which document or table it came from, and when. Without provenance you can show an answer but you can't defend it, and an answer you can't defend is not usable in a regulated process.

  7. Put permissions on the graph, not on the answer

    Filter at retrieval, so a walk simply cannot reach a pin this user isn't entitled to see. Trying to censor the model's output afterwards is a losing game.

At work

A sensible first slice: one segment, one product family, six node types, provenance from day one. Ship a graph that answers five questions properly rather than one that half-answers fifty. And run it beside your existing vector search, not instead of it.

What's next

So what is Graph RAG?

RAG means retrieval-augmented generation: before the model answers, go and fetch the relevant facts and hand them over. Graph RAG is RAG where the fetching walks a knowledge graph instead of only grabbing lookalike notes.

So yes — it's related to both of the things - Vector DBs and Knowledge Graphs. It doesn't replace them. It uses them. The pile finds the door. The map walks the corridors. The model does the talking.

Watch the same question go three ways.

At work

The middle lane is where most enterprise assistants are stuck today, and it's why they demo well and disappoint in production. Nothing is broken — the joining fact just wasn't in the chunks that came back. Graph RAG makes the join deterministic instead of hoping it fits in the context window.

What it's for

What Graph RAG actually powers.

  • Questions whose answer lives in five places at once. The fact is never in one document. Retrieval by similarity gets you four of the five pieces and a confident guess about the fifth.
  • Answers with receipts. The path is the citation. You can hand someone the chain of facts the answer stands on, which is the difference between a demo and something an advisor is allowed to use.
  • "How are these connected?" Related parties, shared exposures, rings, referral chains, who-touched-what. Fraud and compliance teams have wanted this for years and it was always a query problem, not an AI problem.
  • Big-picture summaries. Group the graph into clusters, summarise each cluster once, and you can answer what are the themes across these 4,000 documents — a question ordinary retrieval cannot even begin, because there is no single chunk that contains the answer.
  • Next best action. A nudge is an argument: this client, this holding, this event, therefore this suggestion. Those are edges. A graph lets the reasoning be inspected instead of inferred.
  • Agents that don't wander. Give an agent a graph and its moves are constrained to real relationships, which is a much cheaper way to keep it honest than more prompt instructions.

Which should I use?

Pile only

Search, FAQs, "find me something like this", summarising one document at a time. Cheap, fast, and genuinely enough for a lot of things. Start here.

Map only

You already know the relationships and you need exact answers. Who owns what, who covers whom, what is this client entitled to. No model required — this is a query.

Both

The answer needs facts joined across sources, or somebody will ask you to prove it. That's Graph RAG, and it costs more to build and keep fresh than either half alone. Add the map when your questions start needing joins, not before.

Maya's neighbourhood is made up. Everything it demonstrates isn't. If you remember one thing: the pile tells you what looks alike, the map tells you what's connected, and Graph RAG is what you get when you stop treating that as a choice.
