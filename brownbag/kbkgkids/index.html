A very simple explainer

A board full of string

How computers remember things, why some of what they remember goes stale, and how they follow a piece of string to find an answer.

Two ways to remember

Imagine you want to remember everything about your neighbourhood. You could do it in two ways, and they are not the same.

A big box of notes

You write a note about everything that happens and drop it in a box. When you want to know something, you dig through the box for notes that sound like your question.

This works well. But every note sits on its own. Nothing in the box knows that the note about Mia and the note about the library are about the same afternoon.

A board with pins and string

You put a pin in a board for every person, every place and every thing. Then you tie a string between two pins whenever they go together, and you write on the string why.

Now you can start at one pin and walk along the strings to somewhere else. The answer is in the walk.

Both are useful, and most of the time the box is enough. If you ask "what happened at the library on Tuesday?", the box has a note that says so. Digging works.

But now ask something harder. "Who might like the new dinosaur book?" There is no note in the box that says that. Nobody ever wrote it down, because it never happened yet. You have to work it out by starting at the book, walking to dinosaurs, and then walking to whoever likes dinosaurs.

The box can only give back what someone already wrote. The board can give back things nobody wrote, because the answer was hiding in how the strings join up.

What grown-ups call these The box of notes is a knowledge base. The board of pins and string is a knowledge graph. Most useful systems keep both, because each one is good at what the other is bad at.

Some strings get old

Not everything you tie onto the board stays true.

If Leo borrowed a book yesterday, that string is fresh. It tells you a lot about what Leo is into right now.

If Leo borrowed a book two years ago, the string is still there. It is still true that it happened. But it does not tell you much about Leo today. He might not even remember it.

So old strings should go faint. Not cut, just faint. You still want to be able to look back and see what the board said last year, so you never snip anything off. You just let the old ones fade when you are working out what matters now.

And here is the bit people get wrong. Different kinds of string go faint at different speeds. "Leo clicked on this" goes faint in a couple of weeks. "Leo is Sam's brother" never goes faint at all. If you fade everything at the same speed, you will forget the things that were meant to last.

What grown-ups call this Letting old connections count for less is temporal decay. The trick is not the fading itself, it is deciding how fast each kind of thing should fade.

Putting things on a map

Computers are not good at words. They are very good at numbers. So they cheat.

They give every single thing a spot on a big invisible map, and they put things that go together close to each other. Then "what is like this?" becomes the easiest question in the world. You just look at what is nearby.

But here is the surprise. There is more than one way to decide what "close" means, and you get a completely different map each time.

Make the map from what things say, and the cake book lands next to the bread book, because they use the same words. Make the map from who touches them, and the cake book lands next to Ada, because Ada is the only one who ever borrows it.

Neither map is the right one. They are answers to different questions. Look at only one and you will miss half of what is going on.

What grown-ups call this A spot on one of these maps is an embedding. There are several kinds, they live on separate maps, and you cannot measure a distance between two things that are on different maps.

When the string isn't there

Every board has holes in it. Somebody forgot to tie a string, or nobody had noticed yet that two things went together.

This causes a very particular kind of trouble. If you walk the board looking for an answer and the string is missing, you do not get a wrong answer. You get nothing at all, and nothing looks exactly the same as "there is no answer."

That is worse than being wrong, because nobody notices. The board goes quiet and everyone assumes it had nothing to say.

So you keep the box of notes underneath, as a safety net. Digging through notes always gives you something. It might be a bit off, but it is never silent.

One rule matters a lot here. When an answer comes from walking the strings, you can show the little story of how you got there. When it comes from digging through the box, you cannot. So you must always remember which way you found a thing, and never let a dug-up answer pretend it walked.

Guessing a string that isn't there

Once you have looked at enough of the board, you start to notice patterns.

Everyone who likes space also borrows the moon book. Everyone who visits the bakery also likes baking. After a while you can look at a pin with a missing string and make a pretty good guess about where that string ought to go.

Computers can do this too, and they are surprisingly good at it. They can suggest a string that nobody ever tied.

But a guess is a guess. It has no little story behind it. If somebody asks "why did you say that?", the honest answer is "because it felt about right", and that is not good enough when the answer matters.

So the sensible thing is to let the guess suggest, and then go looking for a real path on the board that backs it up. If you find one, lovely, show the path. If you don't, either say out loud that it was only a hunch, or don't say it at all.

Two more things to know A guesser only knows the pins it has already seen. Add a brand new person to the board this morning and the guesser has nothing to say about them until it has had another look at everything. And a guesser is a photograph — it remembers the board as it was, not as it is.

Dropping a stone in a pond

Here is another way to use a board, and it is the prettiest one.

Start at everything one person has ever done. Drop a stone on each of those pins at the same time. Let the ripples spread outward along the strings.

Where two ripples cross, that spot gets a bigger wave. That is the whole idea. If something is reachable from several different things you already know about a person, it is probably a better answer than something reachable from only one.

Ripples get weaker the further they travel, so things a long way off barely register. That is right. Nearly everything is connected to nearly everything if you walk far enough, and most of those connections mean nothing.

ripples spreading from what we already know
Go out far enough and everything is reachable, which means "reachable" stops meaning anything. Knowing when to stop is most of the skill.

Three ways of looking, at once

So now there are three ways to find something, and each one is bad at exactly what the others are good at.

Walk the strings

Always comes with a little story you can show someone. Goes completely silent whenever a string is missing, without telling you that's what happened.

Dig through the notes

Never comes back empty, and works on things that arrived five minutes ago. Can never tell you why it picked what it picked.

Guess from patterns

Reaches things nobody ever wrote down or tied up. Has no reason behind it, and does not know about anything new.

The trick is not to pick one. The trick is to run all three, put everything they found into one pile, and then sort the pile carefully — remembering, for each thing in it, which of the three found it.

That last bit is the part everyone skips, and it is the important one. A thing found by walking can show its story. A thing found by guessing cannot. If you let them get muddled in the pile, a hunch walks out the door wearing an explanation it never earned.

What grown-ups call this Using the board to decide what gets looked at, instead of only digging through the box, is called GraphRAG. All the interesting arguments about it are arguments about how to sort the pile.

Lots of helpers, one board

Now imagine you do not have one clever helper. You have several, and each one is only good at a single job.

One helper is good at reading new things and pinning them up. One is good at walking the strings to find people. One is good at checking whether an idea is actually allowed. One is good at writing the whole thing up nicely at the end.

They pass work along to each other, like people in a kitchen. And they all look at the same board.

Which leads to the one thing that really matters here, and it is not about computers at all.

They have to agree on what the words mean. If one helper thinks "friend" means anyone you have ever met, and another thinks it means someone you see every week, they will be perfectly polite to each other and produce complete nonsense. Passing messages around is easy. Agreeing what the messages mean is the hard part, and the board is where that agreement lives.

four helpers, one board, one person deciding
The person at the top is not decoration. Somebody has to be able to say no, and whatever they decide should go back onto the board so that next time is a bit better.
What grown-ups call this Helpers handing work sideways to other helpers is agent-to-agent. A helper reaching down to look something up is tool use. The agreed list of what all the words mean is an ontology, and it is boring, and it is the thing that decides whether any of the rest of it works.

The whole thing in six lines

If you remember nothing else.

A box of notes gives you back what somebody wrote down.

A board of string gives you back things nobody wrote down, because the answer was in the joins.

Old strings should go faint, and different kinds should fade at different speeds.

Three ways of finding things, all running at once, beats any one of them — as long as you remember which one found what.

A guess with no story behind it is fine, right up until somebody asks why.

And the hardest part was never the board. It was getting everyone to agree what the words meant.

The board on this page is real. The pins push each other apart, the strings pull them together, and the fading is worked out as you drag the slider. Everything else here is a neighbourhood that does not exist.
