Brown bag

The History of GenAI

How it built up, developed, and became mainstream so quickly. Spoiler: it didn't happen quickly. It took about eighty years, and we only saw the last six months of it.

The short answers

If you only have five minutes before the sandwiches run out.

What was the buildup?

Eighty years of neural network research that failed twice, plus three separate infrastructure revolutions that had nothing to do with AI: video game GPUs, the crawlable web, and cloud computing. GenAI is what happened when those three finished arriving at the same time.

Was it the Transformer?

It was necessary but not sufficient. The Transformer's real gift was not that it understood language better — it was that it removed the sequential bottleneck, so a training run could be spread across thousands of chips at once. It made scale affordable rather than making models smart. Five years passed between the paper and ChatGPT, which tells you the architecture alone was not the unlock.

Was something earlier bigger?

Yes, two things. 2012, when AlexNet proved that learning from data at scale beat decades of hand-engineered features — that was the regime change, and everything since is a consequence. And self-supervised pretraining, which removed the need for labelled data and so made the entire internet a training set.

Why did it go mainstream during COVID?

It mostly didn't — ChatGPT launched in November 2022, after the acute phase had passed. But the research-to-product pipeline ran entirely through the pandemic, and the era's free money, cloud buildout and digital-first habits materially accelerated it. Correlation with a real mechanism attached, not coincidence and not cause.

Is OpenAI the pioneer?

No, and also yes. Google invented most of the parts and chose not to ship. OpenAI invented comparatively little but was right about one enormous thing — that scaling would keep working — and was willing to put it in front of the public for free. Invention and arrival are different achievements, and OpenAI owns the second one.

Five questions, five unlocks

The cleanest way to hold the whole history in your head. Building a generative model required answering five separate questions, and each was answered by a different breakthrough in a different decade. None of them alone would have produced ChatGPT.

Question 1

With what?

CUDA, 2006 onward

NVIDIA opened up graphics chips for general computation. Hardware built to render video games turned out to be almost perfectly shaped for the matrix multiplication that neural networks are made of. Nobody designed it that way.

Question 2

On what?

ImageNet 2009, self-supervision 2013–18

First a large labelled dataset proved scale mattered. Then self-supervised pretraining removed the need for labels entirely — if the training signal is just "predict the next word," every document ever written becomes training data for free.

Question 3

How?

Transformer, 2017

Recurrent models processed text one word at a time, which no amount of hardware could speed up. Self-attention looks at every position simultaneously, so training parallelises across thousands of chips. This is what made a nine-figure training run physically possible.

Question 4

How much?

Scaling laws, 2020

OpenAI showed that loss falls predictably as compute, data and parameters increase. That converted an open research question into a budgeting exercise: spend X and you get Y. It is the single most consequential idea for why billions got spent.

Question 5

For whom?

RLHF and instruction tuning, 2017–22

A raw pretrained model predicts likely text, which is not the same as being helpful. Training on human preference rankings closed that gap. The most under-appreciated step, and the one that actually made the product work.

Architecture answered "how". Self-supervision answered "on what". Everything else was economics.

Your question 2 and 3

Was it the Transformer — or was something earlier bigger?

The Transformer gets the credit because it is in the name. GPT literally stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformer. But ranking the milestones by how much of the outcome they explain gives a different order.

1

The 2012 convergence — AlexNet, ImageNet, CUDA

On 30 September 2012, a neural network called AlexNet won the ImageNet competition by an enormous margin against systems built on decades of hand-crafted computer vision features. Nothing in it was conceptually new — convolutional networks dated to the late 1980s, GPUs had been used for training since around 2006. What was new was the combination: a big dataset, enough parallel compute, and a deep network trained end to end. It ended the argument about whether learned representations beat engineered ones, and every subsequent development is downstream of that answer.

The regime change. Not a better model — a different theory of how to build software that works.
2

Self-supervised pretraining

The quiet giant. Supervised learning needs labelled examples, and labels are expensive, so dataset size caps model quality. Self-supervision removes the cap: hide part of the input and make the model predict it. Word vectors in 2013 showed that useful structure could be learned this way; GPT-1 and BERT in 2018 showed that a single pretrained model could then be adapted to almost any task. This is what turned "the entire internet" into a training set, and it is why generative models exist at all — a model trained to predict the next token is a generator, for free, as a side effect.

The bigger milestone most people skip. Without it the Transformer would have had nothing to eat.
3

The Transformer, 2017

Eight Google researchers published a paper arguing that the recurrence everyone was using could be thrown away and replaced entirely with attention. The headline benefit is usually described as better handling of long-range context, which is true. The more important benefit is structural: recurrent networks must process a sequence in order, so they cannot be parallelised across time, and throwing more GPUs at them does not help much. A Transformer processes every position at once. That single property is what made it economically rational to spend tens of millions of dollars on one training run — and it is why the same architecture later swallowed images, audio, video and protein folding too.

Necessary, and the most load-bearing single architecture. But an enabler of scale, not the source of intelligence.
4

Scaling laws, 2020

A team at OpenAI published empirical curves showing model performance improving smoothly and predictably with more compute, data and parameters — no plateau in sight. DeepMind's Chinchilla work in 2022 corrected the recipe toward training smaller models on far more data, which mattered enormously for cost, because a smaller model is cheaper to run forever afterwards. Together these turned "will it get better if we spend more?" from a research gamble into a forecastable return.

Why the money showed up. Investors cannot fund a hunch, but they can fund a curve.
5

RLHF and instruction tuning, 2017–2022

Reinforcement learning from human preferences was published in 2017, from a collaboration involving OpenAI and DeepMind. Applied to language models in InstructGPT in early 2022, the results were startling: a 1.3-billion-parameter model tuned this way was preferred by human raters over the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 it came from. A model more than a hundred times smaller won, because it had been taught what people actually wanted rather than merely what text was likely. ChatGPT is GPT-3.5 plus this technique plus a chat box.

The last mile, and the one that made the difference between a research demo and a billion users.

The honest verdict

If you want one answer for the room: the Transformer is the most important single invention, but 2012 is the more important date. 2012 decided the paradigm — learning beats engineering — and the Transformer decided how far that paradigm could be pushed before physics and budgets intervened. Asking which matters more is a bit like asking whether the internal combustion engine or the discovery of oil mattered more for cars.

The timeline

Filter by track to see how five separate threads — research, compute, data, product and capital — converged. Highlighted entries are the pivots.

  1. 1943research

    The artificial neuron

    McCulloch and Pitts describe a mathematical model of a neuron. The idea that thinking might be computation over simple connected units predates the computer itself.

  2. 1958research

    The perceptron

    Rosenblatt builds a machine that learns its own weights from examples rather than being programmed. The press coverage is wildly overheated. A critique in 1969 helps trigger the first AI winter.

  3. 1966product

    ELIZA

    Weizenbaum's pattern-matching therapist. People formed emotional attachments to a few hundred lines of rules, which should have warned everyone about what a conversational interface does to human judgement.

  4. 1986research

    Backpropagation goes mainstream

    Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams popularise an efficient way to assign blame across the layers of a deep network. This is the mathematical engine underneath every model discussed here, forty years later.

  5. 1989–98research

    Convolutional networks

    LeCun's networks read handwritten digits well enough to process a significant share of US bank cheques. Proof that neural networks could do real commercial work — on a narrow task, at small scale.

  6. 1997research

    LSTM

    Hochreiter and Schmidhuber solve the problem of networks forgetting across long sequences. LSTMs would dominate language modelling for the next twenty years, and power translation and speech products at Google and Apple.

  7. 2006–07compute

    NVIDIA releases CUDA

    General-purpose programming for graphics cards. This is the accidental keystone of the whole story: a gaming company opens its hardware to scientific computing, and a decade later that decision underwrites a multi-trillion-dollar industry.

  8. 2009data

    ImageNet

    Fei-Fei Li's team assembles millions of labelled images, built partly through crowdsourced human labour. A dataset, not an algorithm — and the field's central bottleneck for the next decade turns out to be data, not ideas.

  9. Sep 2012research

    AlexNet wins ImageNet

    Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton win by roughly ten percentage points using a deep network trained on two consumer GPUs. The field reorganises around deep learning within about eighteen months. Sutskever later co-founds OpenAI.

  10. 2013research

    Word2vec

    Mikolov's team at Google learns vector representations of words from raw text with no labels. Early, concrete proof that self-supervision on abundant text yields genuinely useful structure.

  11. 2014research

    Sequence-to-sequence, attention, and GANs

    Three at once. Google's seq2seq maps one sequence to another, enabling neural translation. Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio add attention so the model can look back at relevant input. And Goodfellow's GANs make machines generate convincing images for the first time — the beginning of generative AI as a visible thing.

  12. Dec 2015capital

    OpenAI is founded

    A nonprofit created partly in response to fears about Google's dominance in AI and about existential risk — a worry articulated by Bostrom's Superintelligence, published the previous year. Its founding bet is that scale is the path.

  13. 2015research

    Diffusion models proposed

    Sohl-Dickstein's group describes generating images by learning to reverse a noising process. Ignored for five years until improvements in 2020 make it work — and then it displaces GANs entirely.

  14. 2016product

    AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol

    DeepMind's system wins a game considered a decade away from solution. The first time deep learning genuinely frightened the general public — and it convinced a lot of executives to fund AI groups.

  15. 2016compute

    Google's TPU

    Google builds custom silicon specifically for neural networks. The start of an arms race in purpose-built AI hardware that is now the binding constraint on the entire industry.

  16. Jun 2017research

    "Attention Is All You Need"

    Eight researchers at Google publish the Transformer. Recurrence is discarded; everything is attention. Crucially, training now parallelises across chips. Every one of the eight authors would eventually leave Google, most to found or join AI startups.

  17. 2017research

    Deep RL from human preferences

    Christiano and colleagues, in work spanning OpenAI and DeepMind, show that a model can be trained from human comparisons of its outputs rather than from a hand-written reward. Five years later this becomes the technique that makes ChatGPT pleasant to talk to.

  18. 2018research

    GPT-1 and BERT

    OpenAI's Alec Radford shows that pretraining a Transformer on unlabelled text, then fine-tuning, beats task-specific models. Google's BERT does the same with a different objective and dominates the benchmarks. The pretrain-then-adapt era begins. Notably, BERT was the bigger deal at the time.

  19. Feb 2019product

    GPT-2 and the staged release

    OpenAI declines to release the full model immediately, citing misuse risk. Widely mocked as a publicity stunt, but it establishes the template of AI capability as newsworthy — and teaches OpenAI how much attention a language model could attract.

  20. Jul 2019capital

    Microsoft invests $1bn in OpenAI

    OpenAI restructures to a capped-profit model to raise it. The money buys compute, and Microsoft becomes the exclusive cloud provider. Without this, the scaling bet is unaffordable and the story ends here.

  21. Jan 2020research

    Scaling laws for neural language models

    Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI publish smooth, predictable relationships between compute, data, model size and performance. This is the document that turns AI research into an investment thesis.

  22. Jun 2020product

    GPT-3 and the API

    175 billion parameters, and the surprise that it could perform new tasks from a few examples in the prompt with no retraining. Released as a commercial API rather than a paper. Developers were amazed; the public barely registered it. This is the moment the capability existed and nobody noticed.

  23. 2020research

    Diffusion models start working

    Ho and colleagues make denoising diffusion produce high-quality images. Within two years this underpins DALL·E 2, Midjourney and Stable Diffusion, and the image side of generative AI reaches the public before the text side does.

  24. Jan 2021product

    DALL·E and CLIP

    OpenAI connects language and images. CLIP — which learns from image-caption pairs scraped at scale — becomes the steering mechanism for nearly every text-to-image system that follows, including open-source ones.

  25. Jun 2021product

    GitHub Copilot

    The first genuinely mainstream generative AI product, and it went to developers. Quietly proved that people would pay monthly for a model that completes their work, eighteen months before the world noticed.

  26. 2020–21product

    Google builds Meena, then LaMDA, then shelves both

    Google Brain's chatbot is demonstrated internally and impresses. Leadership repeatedly declines to release it publicly, citing safety and fairness standards and, implicitly, the risk to search. Its two lead researchers leave the company in frustration. This decision is the hinge of the whole story.

  27. Early 2022research

    InstructGPT

    OpenAI applies human-feedback training to GPT-3. A 1.3B-parameter version is preferred by human raters to the 175B original. Alignment turns out to be more cost-effective than a hundredfold increase in size. The product becomes possible.

  28. Mar 2022research

    Chinchilla

    DeepMind shows that the field had been building models too large and training them on too little data. The corrected recipe yields smaller, cheaper, better models — which is exactly what you need if you intend to serve one free to the entire internet.

  29. Apr–Aug 2022product

    DALL·E 2, Midjourney, Stable Diffusion

    Image generation goes viral across three products in five months. Stability AI releases Stable Diffusion's weights openly in August, which anyone can run on a home GPU. The public learns the phrase "generative AI" here — months before ChatGPT.

  30. 30 Nov 2022product

    ChatGPT

    Released as a low-key "research preview" of a model that already existed. Free, no waitlist, no API key, no prompt engineering required. One million users in five days; one hundred million in two months, the fastest consumer adoption on record. The technology was eighteen months old. The interface was new.

  31. Dec 2022 – 2023capital

    Code red, and the race

    Google declares an internal emergency and rushes Bard out. Microsoft puts GPT-4 into Bing. Meta releases LLaMA, and its leak seeds the open-weights ecosystem. Every large company on earth acquires an AI strategy within about six months.

  32. 2024–26capital

    The infrastructure supercycle

    Attention shifts from models to the physical plant: chips, data centres, power. Hyperscaler capital expenditure runs into the hundreds of billions annually, and electricity supply becomes the binding constraint. Meanwhile reasoning models and agents shift the frontier from knowing things to doing things.

Your core question

Why it felt sudden when it wasn't

The capability curve was smooth. The access curve was a cliff. Four things happened between GPT-3 in June 2020 and ChatGPT in November 2022, and none of them was a breakthrough in intelligence.

  • The model learned to take instructions. GPT-3 was a text completer. Ask it a question and it might continue with more questions, because that is what a page of questions looks like on the internet. Getting useful output required knowing how to construct prompts. Human-feedback training closed that gap, and the difference between "powerful if you know how to hold it" and "works when you type a sentence" is the entire difference between a research artefact and a product.
  • Inference got cheap enough to give away. Serving a model to millions of people for free is a per-query cost, forever. The Chinchilla correction, quantisation, better serving infrastructure and falling compute prices moved free consumer access from insane to merely very expensive. This is an underrated part of the timing.
  • The interface removed every barrier at once. No waitlist, no credit card, no API key, no documentation, no installation. A text box on a web page. Compare with GPT-3, which had the same underlying capability but sat behind a developer signup — and reached a tiny fraction of the audience. The conversational frame also did something subtler: it told people what the thing was for, which a completion API never did.
  • The incumbent held back and the challenger did not. Google had equivalent technology and repeatedly declined to release it, because a chatbot that says something wrong is a brand crisis for a company whose product is answers, and because answering questions directly cannibalises the search business it funds everything with. OpenAI had no search revenue to protect and no brand to damage. This is a textbook innovator's dilemma, and it is why the company that invented the Transformer was second to market with it.

The pattern worth naming: capability overhang

For roughly two years, a capability existed that almost nobody was using, because the last mile of usability had not been built. When it was built, adoption did not grow — it detonated, because all the latent demand released at once.

This is not unique to AI. It is the same shape as the graphical interface versus the command line, or SMS versus WhatsApp. But it is worth internalising, because it means the useful question about any AI capability is rarely "does it exist?" — it is "what is still between it and a person who could use it?" That gap is usually interface, trust and cost, not intelligence. And it can close very fast.

Your question 4

The COVID question

Worth separating the timing from the causation, because the popular version of this story is slightly wrong in a way that matters.

First, the correction. Generative AI did not go mainstream during the pandemic. ChatGPT launched on 30 November 2022, by which point most of the world had returned to something like normal life. What is true is that the entire pipeline from research result to shipped product ran inside the pandemic window: scaling laws in January 2020, GPT-3 that June, DALL·E in January 2021, Copilot that June, then the image-generation wave through 2022. The work was done in lockdown. The unveiling came after.

Second, the real mechanisms. COVID was not the cause, but it was a genuine accelerant, through four channels that are easy to miss.

What COVID actually contributed

  • Extremely cheap money. The 2020–21 monetary response produced one of the largest risk-capital surges in history. Stability AI, Midjourney, Anthropic, Cohere, Hugging Face and others were funded in that window. AI research is unusually capital-hungry and this was the cheapest capital anyone had ever seen.
  • A forced cloud buildout. Overnight, every organisation on earth needed remote infrastructure. Hyperscalers expanded aggressively, and the GPU capacity that expansion created was available when model training demand arrived.
  • Digital-first became the default behaviour. Two years of doing everything through a browser tab lowered the psychological barrier to a new tool that also lived in a browser tab. A conversational assistant landed on a population newly habituated to talking to software.
  • The 2022 crypto collapse. Often forgotten. It freed up GPU supply and, more importantly, left a large pool of technology capital and attention looking for the next narrative. Generative AI arrived almost exactly on cue.

What it didn't contribute

  • Any of the science. The Transformer, self-supervised pretraining, scaling laws, diffusion and RLHF were all either published or well underway before March 2020. None of the intellectual work was pandemic-driven.
  • The timing of the launch. ChatGPT shipped when it did because InstructGPT worked, because serving costs had fallen, and — by several accounts — because OpenAI was worried about being beaten to market. Not because of anything epidemiological.
  • The adoption spike. The explosion happened in early 2023, in a world that was back in offices. If lockdown boredom were the driver, the curve would have peaked eighteen months earlier.

The fair summary for a brown bag: COVID did not create generative AI, but it did fund it, build the data centres for it, and train the public to live inside a browser tab. It compressed the timeline by perhaps a year or two. It did not cause the thing.

Your question 5

Is OpenAI really who we have to thank?

The most contested question in the set, and the one where the popular answer is furthest from the record. Here is the ledger.

WhoWhat they contributedWhere the credit actually sits
Hinton, LeCun, Bengio Backpropagation in practice, convolutional networks, and the stubbornness to keep working on neural networks through two decades in which the field considered them a dead end. Foundational. Turing Award in 2018, before most people had heard of any of this.
Hochreiter & Schmidhuber LSTM, which made sequence modelling work at all and powered translation and speech for two decades before Transformers. Essential predecessor, chronically under-credited.
Fei-Fei Li and the ImageNet team The dataset that made the 2012 breakthrough measurable, plus the insight that data — not algorithms — was the binding constraint. Made the paradigm shift legible. Often left out entirely.
NVIDIA CUDA, and a decade of investment in making graphics hardware programmable for science before there was any AI market to justify it. The accidental keystone. None of this runs without it.
Google Word2vec, sequence-to-sequence, the Transformer, BERT, TPUs, T5, foundational diffusion work, and a working conversational assistant in Meena and LaMDA years before ChatGPT. Invented more of generative AI than anyone else, and shipped almost none of it. Declined to release its chatbot on safety, fairness and business-risk grounds.
Montreal / Bahdanau, Cho, Bengio The attention mechanism itself, in 2014, three years before the Transformer generalised it. The idea in "Attention Is All You Need" is partly theirs.
Goodfellow; Sohl-Dickstein and Ho GANs, then diffusion — the two families that made image generation work. Diffusion sat ignored for five years before anyone got it to produce good pictures. The entire visual half of generative AI.
OpenAI Comparatively few new architectures. What it did contribute: conviction that scaling would keep working when most of the field doubted it; the empirical scaling laws; CLIP; productionised RLHF; and the decision to put a free chat interface in front of the entire public. Not the inventor. The one that was right about scale, and the one that shipped.
Microsoft The capital and compute that made the scaling bet affordable, starting with a billion dollars in 2019. Without this, OpenAI's conviction stays theoretical.
Stability AI, Meta, the open-source community Open weights for Stable Diffusion and LLaMA, which broke the assumption that frontier capability had to live behind an API and seeded an entire independent ecosystem. Why there is a market rather than a monopoly.
Everyone who ever published anything online, and the annotators The training corpus, contributed without consent or payment. Plus the low-paid workers in Kenya, Venezuela and elsewhere who labelled data and built the content filters that made these systems shippable. The largest and least acknowledged contribution in the whole ledger.

The verdict

OpenAI is the pioneer of generative AI as a product, not as a technology. If the question is who invented the machinery, the honest answer is a diffuse community of researchers over eighty years, with Google contributing more of the decisive pieces than any other single organisation. If the question is who made it arrive, that is OpenAI, and the credit is real — being right about scaling when your entire field is sceptical is a genuine intellectual achievement, not just a marketing one, and shipping to the public is a decision with real risk attached.

The useful framing is that invention and distribution are different skills, and the second one is what the world experiences. Xerox PARC invented the graphical interface; Apple and Microsoft delivered it. Nobody remembers PARC. Google is currently living through the same thing with a paper it published in 2017.

What to take away

Five points that survive the details, and that generalise beyond this one technology.

  1. Nothing about this was sudden except the distribution

    Eighty years of research, fifteen years of compute buildout, ten years of the deep learning paradigm, five years of Transformers. The discontinuity in late 2022 was in access, not capability. When you hear that an AI capability "appeared overnight," the correct response is to ask how long it had been sitting in a paper.

  2. The unlocks were mostly economic, not scientific

    Cheap parallel compute, free training data via self-supervision, a parallelisable architecture, a predictable return curve, and falling inference cost. Four of those five are about cost and scale rather than about a new idea. Progress in this field has been bought at least as much as it has been discovered.

  3. The last mile is where the value was created

    A 1.3-billion-parameter model tuned on human feedback beat a 175-billion-parameter model that wasn't. The gap between a capable system and a usable one was worth more than a hundredfold increase in raw scale. Applies directly to anything you are building: the model is rarely the hard part.

  4. Incumbency is a real handicap here

    Google had the technology, the talent, the data and the compute, and lost the market anyway — because it had a profitable business to protect and a brand that could be damaged. Careful institutions under-ship. That is worth remembering both as a warning and as a reason the caution had some merit.

  5. Watch the overhang, not the headlines

    The most predictable thing about the next few years is that some capability which already exists in a paper or an internal demo will suddenly become universal because somebody removes the friction. The forecasting question is not what will be invented. It is what already works and is still too awkward, expensive, or reputationally risky to put in front of people.

Invention makes the headlines. Distribution makes the history.
